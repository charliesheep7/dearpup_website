## The Short Answer

Yes, dogs can eat clams — with three conditions. The clams need to be **fully cooked, completely out of the shell, and served plain**. Meet those three and a small portion of clam meat is a perfectly reasonable treat for most healthy dogs.

What makes clams risky is almost never the clam itself. It's the garlic butter they were cooked in, the shell fragments left in the bowl, or the fact that your dog found them on a beach instead of in your kitchen. Sort those out and the rest is simple.

## Why Clams Are Actually Decent for Dogs

Clams are lean shellfish — mostly protein and water, with very little fat. They're a natural source of the same nutrients that make other seafood worth feeding: high-quality protein, omega-3 fatty acids, zinc, and iron.

That said, keep expectations grounded. **A tablespoon of clam meat is a treat, not a supplement.** If your dog eats a complete and balanced diet, they're already getting these nutrients in the right proportions. Clams are a nice flavor bonus for a picky eater, not a nutritional fix.

The one nutrient worth flagging is iodine. Shellfish is naturally rich in it, and while iodine is essential in small amounts, [PetMD notes](https://www.petmd.com/dog/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-crab) that shellfish meat is high in sodium, cholesterol, and iodine — none of which your dog needs in quantity. This is one more reason the portion stays small.

## The Real Risks Worth Knowing

### Raw clams carry bacteria

This is the big one. Uncooked shellfish can harbor bacteria and parasites that a healthy dog's stomach isn't equipped to handle. *Vibrio parahaemolyticus* is the usual suspect — it lives naturally in coastal saltwater and causes an estimated [35,000 foodborne infections a year in the United States](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5779391/), most of them traced to raw or undercooked shellfish.

Thorough cooking handles this. Steam, boil, or bake clams until they open fully, and skip anything served raw on the half shell.

### Shells cause real damage

Clam shells are sharp, hard, and completely indigestible. As PetMD puts it in the context of crab shells, a shell can injure a dog's mouth and intestinal tract, and it can also cause choking or an intestinal blockage. Small dogs are at the highest risk simply because the same shell fragment is proportionally much bigger inside them.

Pick through the meat with your fingers before serving. It takes ten seconds and removes the single most likely way clams send a dog to the emergency vet.

### The seasoning is usually the problem

Clams rarely arrive plain. They come steamed in white wine, swimming in garlic butter, packed in salty brine, or buried in chowder. Garlic and onion are genuinely toxic to dogs, alcohol is off the table entirely, and heavy cream and butter are a fast route to an upset stomach — or pancreatitis in susceptible dogs.

If you're feeding your dog clams, cook a few separately with nothing on them. The version on your plate isn't the version for their bowl. Our guide to [foods that are toxic to dogs](/blog/toxic-foods-for-dogs) covers the seasonings worth memorizing.

## Beach Clams Are a Completely Different Story

Everything above assumes clams from a kitchen. Clams from a shoreline are a separate problem, and a much bigger one.

Clams are filter feeders, so they concentrate whatever is in the water around them — including toxins produced by algal blooms. Those toxins, saxitoxins among them, **survive cooking and freezing**, which means the usual "just cook it" advice does nothing here.

The consequences are documented. A [2018 study published in *Toxins*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5869382/) investigated a cluster of dog poisonings on beaches in Norfolk and Suffolk, England, after winter storms washed marine life ashore. Over roughly two weeks, nine dogs fell ill and two died. Symptoms included vomiting, loss of motor control, and muscle paralysis — one dog lost consciousness within an hour of eating something off the sand. Testing found saxitoxin concentrations above 14,000 µg per kilogram in one sample.

The practical rule is simple: **on the beach, your dog eats nothing they find.** Washed-up clams, crabs, starfish, and dead fish are all off limits, however appealing they smell. If your dog is a determined beach scavenger, a long line or a basket muzzle is a reasonable investment.

## How to Serve Clams Safely

If you want to share, here's the whole process:

1. **Buy fresh or frozen, not canned.** Canned clams are usually packed in salt. If canned is what you have, choose ones packed in water with no salt added and rinse them thoroughly.
2. **Cook them plain.** Steam or boil until the shells open. No butter, oil, garlic, onion, wine, or salt.
3. **Remove every trace of shell.** Pull the meat out and check it with your fingers.
4. **Let it cool completely**, then chop it into small pieces.
5. **Serve a small portion.** For shellfish, PetMD suggests roughly **1 tablespoon per 10 pounds of body weight, once or twice a week at most.** A 20-pound Beagle gets about two tablespoons; a 60-pound Lab gets about a third of a cup.
6. **Start with less than that the first time** and wait 24 hours to see how your dog handles it.

Like all treats, clams should fit inside the 10% rule — no more than a tenth of your dog's daily calories from anything that isn't their complete diet. If you're not sure what that number is for your dog, our [dog calorie calculator](/blog/dog-calorie-calculator) works it out.

## Signs of Trouble and When to Call Your Vet

Most dogs who eat a little cooked clam are completely fine. Watch for these signs anyway, especially the first time:

- Vomiting or diarrhea
- Excessive gas or an obviously uncomfortable belly
- Itching, hives, or facial swelling — shellfish allergies in dogs are uncommon but real
- Drooling, gagging, or pawing at the mouth, which can signal a shell fragment

**Call your vet right away** if your dog swallowed shell and then vomits repeatedly, refuses food, has a hard or painful abdomen, or strains without producing stool. Those point toward an obstruction, which doesn't resolve on its own.

Treat any of the following as an emergency: stumbling, weakness in the back legs, tremors, or collapse after your dog ate shellfish — particularly anything scavenged outdoors. These are the neurological signs seen in shellfish toxin cases, and they move fast.

## Practical Takeaways

- Cooked, shelled, plain clam meat is safe for most dogs in small amounts
- Raw clams are not — cooking is what removes the bacterial risk
- Shells are the most common cause of real injury, so remove all of them
- Butter, garlic, onion, wine, and salt are the actual hazards in most clam dishes
- Anything your dog finds on a beach is off limits, cooked or not
- Keep it to about 1 tablespoon per 10 pounds, once or twice a week
