## The Short Answer

**Yes, most dogs can eat a little plain ricotta cheese.** It is not toxic, and many dogs think it is the best thing to come out of the fridge. The catch is the same as with any cheese: it is a treat, not a food group.

[PetMD's guide to cheese for dogs](https://www.petmd.com/dog/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-cheese) says dogs can eat cheese in moderation and that it should make up **no more than 10% of your dog's daily diet**. Plain ricotta fits comfortably inside that rule when you keep portions small.

What it does not fit is your leftover lasagna. More on that below.

## Why Ricotta Can Be a Good Treat

Ricotta is a soft, spoonable cheese, which makes it handy in ways that hard cheese is not.

- **Nutrition.** The [AKC notes that cheese contains protein, calcium, vitamin A, essential fatty acids, and B-complex vitamins](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-cheese/).
- **Pill hiding.** The AKC also mentions cheese as a good way to conceal pills. Ricotta's soft texture wraps around a tablet easily.
- **Food toppers and enrichment.** A thin smear inside a lick mat or a dot on top of kibble can tempt a picky eater.
- **Training rewards.** Trainers often use cheese for treat-motivated dogs, according to the AKC.

Ricotta is not on either the AKC's or PetMD's cheese lists by name, so the safest approach is to apply their general cheese guidance to it. That is what the rest of this guide does.

## The Risks: Fat, Lactose, and Add-Ins

### Fat

The AKC warns that **cheese is high in fat**, and that feeding too much regularly can cause weight gain and obesity. More seriously, it could contribute to pancreatitis, which the AKC describes as a serious and potentially fatal illness in dogs.

That is why both the AKC and PetMD point owners toward lower-fat cheeses such as mozzarella and cottage cheese. If you buy ricotta for your dog, **part-skim ricotta is the closer match to that advice** than whole-milk ricotta.

### Lactose

PetMD notes that most dogs are lactose-intolerant and may react badly to too much cheese. Signs of a dairy overload are usually digestive: gas, loose stool, vomiting, or diarrhea. Some dogs handle small amounts of dairy well, and others react to very little.

### Add-ins

Plain ricotta is one thing. Flavored or prepared ricotta is another. The AKC warns that some cheeses contain herbs or other ingredients that are toxic to dogs, such as **garlic, onions, and chives**. Always check the ingredient list, and skip anything seasoned or sweetened.

## How Much Ricotta Can Dogs Have?

Start with a very small taste, about a fingertip's worth, and wait a day to see how your dog's stomach handles it. If all is well, you can offer small amounts occasionally while keeping all treats within that 10% limit.

PetMD notes that some dogs tolerate lower-fat, lower-sodium, lower-lactose cheeses more often, for example as pill pockets, while dogs with sensitivities or conditions like pancreatitis may need cheese restricted to once a week, once a month, or a few times a year. **Your vet is the best judge of where your dog falls.**

## Ricotta Dishes Dogs Should Not Eat

Most of the ricotta in a typical kitchen is not plain. These dishes are the real problem:

- **Lasagna, stuffed shells, manicotti, and ravioli.** These usually combine ricotta with garlic, onion, herbs, salty sauces, and extra cheese. Our post on [mac and cheese for dogs](/blog/can-dogs-eat-mac-and-cheese) covers why rich, cheesy pasta dishes are a poor fit.
- **Cannoli and ricotta desserts.** These add sugar, and some include chocolate chips. Chocolate is on the [ASPCA's list of people foods to avoid feeding pets](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/people-foods-avoid-feeding-your-pets).
- **Herbed or garlic ricotta spreads.** Any ricotta flavored with garlic, onion, or chives should stay out of the dog bowl.

If your dog snuck a small bite of lasagna, watch for vomiting, diarrhea, or lethargy, and call your vet if you are unsure how much garlic or onion was involved.

## Who Should Skip Ricotta

- **Dogs with a history of pancreatitis** or on a low-fat diet
- **Overweight dogs**, since the extra calories add up
- **Dogs with known dairy sensitivity** or chronic digestive issues
- **Dogs on a prescription diet**, unless your vet approves extras

## Signs Ricotta Did Not Agree With Your Dog

Mild signs usually show up within a day:

- Gas or a gurgling stomach
- Soft stool or diarrhea
- Vomiting

Call your vet if vomiting or diarrhea continues, your dog seems painful or lethargic, or they hunch up and will not eat. Those can be signs of pancreatitis, especially after a large, fatty meal.

## Better Cheese Options for Dogs

If your dog loves cheese, the AKC suggests lower-fat choices such as mozzarella, cottage cheese, and soft goat cheese. See our guides to [cottage cheese](/blog/can-dogs-eat-cottage-cheese), [mozzarella](/blog/can-dogs-eat-mozzarella-cheese), and [cream cheese](/blog/can-dogs-have-cream-cheese), or our overview of [cheese for dogs](/blog/can-dogs-eat-cheese). PetMD notes that **blue cheese should be avoided** because it contains roquefortine.

## Practical Takeaways

- Plain ricotta is safe for most dogs in small, occasional amounts.
- Choose part-skim, unflavored ricotta and check the label for garlic, onion, or chives.
- Keep all treats under 10% of your dog's daily diet.
- Never share lasagna, stuffed pasta, cannoli, or seasoned ricotta spreads.
- Skip it for dogs with pancreatitis, weight issues, or dairy sensitivity.
