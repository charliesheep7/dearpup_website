If you are reading this, you are already doing the hard, loving thing: asking the question honestly instead of hoping it answers itself. There is no clean formula here. But there is a structure vets use, and it turns an impossible feeling into a set of answerable questions.

## When to Put Your Dog Down — The Short Answer

The decision comes down to one thing: **can your dog still have more good days than bad ones, with the care available to you?**

Not "is my dog old." Not "is my dog sick." Dogs live well with arthritis, kidney disease, blindness, and cancer for months or years. The turning point is when treatment stops buying comfort — when the bad days outnumber the good and no adjustment brings them back.

The [AKC](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/knowing-time-say-goodbye-pet-euthanasia/) frames the risk on both sides plainly: wait too long and your dog suffers needlessly; decide too early and you lose time you both still had. The checklist below exists to keep you out of both ditches.

## The Quality-of-Life Scale Vets Actually Use

Veterinary oncologist Dr. Alice Villalobos developed a scale that most vets now reach for. It is remembered as **HHHHHMM** — seven categories, each scored 1 to 10, where 10 is best.

| Category                    | What you are scoring                               |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **H**urt                    | Is pain controlled? Is breathing easy?             |
| **H**unger                  | Is your dog eating enough, willingly?              |
| **H**ydration               | Is your dog drinking, or needing fluids?           |
| **H**ygiene                 | Can they stay clean and dry, free of sores?        |
| **H**appiness               | Do they still show interest, joy, response to you? |
| **M**obility                | Can they get up and move without help?             |
| **M**ore good days than bad | Which way is the ratio tipping?                    |

According to [VCA Animal Hospitals](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/quality-of-life-at-the-end-of-life-for-your-dog), a score above 5 in each category — or a total above 35 out of 70 — generally suggests quality of life is still acceptable and continued care is reasonable. Below that, the conversation shifts.

The scale is not folk wisdom. A [2023 validation study in the journal Animals](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10044252/) tested the HHHHHMM scale with owners and found it a usable, meaningful measure of pet quality of life — and a help to owners processing the decision.

Score it today, then again in a week. **The trend matters more than any single number.**

## The Checklist: 12 Questions to Answer This Week

Work through these honestly. Write the answers down — that record is what you will bring to your vet.

1. Is your dog eating on their own, without coaxing or hand-feeding every meal?
2. Are they drinking enough to stay hydrated?
3. Can they stand up from lying down without help?
4. Can they get outside to relieve themselves?
5. Are they staying clean, or lying in their own waste?
6. Is their pain controlled on the current medication, all day and overnight?
7. Is breathing quiet and comfortable at rest?
8. Do they still greet you, or respond when you come home?
9. Do they still want anything — a walk, a sniff, a favorite toy, a scratch?
10. Are they sleeping restfully, or pacing, panting, and restless at night?
11. Over the last 14 days, were there more good days than bad ones?
12. If your dog could see their own situation, would they choose to keep going?

**A "no" to questions 1 through 7 is about suffering. A "no" to 8 through 12 is about whether your dog is still present in their own life.** Several "no" answers across both groups is the clearest signal there is.

## Signs That Usually Mean It Is Time

Some changes carry more weight than others. [PetMD](https://www.petmd.com/dog/general-health/signs-dog-dying) lists the ones vets take most seriously:

- **Refusing food and water** for more than about 24 hours, especially alongside weight loss
- **Labored or open-mouthed breathing** at rest, or persistent panting with no exertion
- **Loss of mobility** — unable to rise, or collapsing on short walks
- **Incontinence** paired with an inability to move away from the mess
- **Hiding, disorientation, or not recognizing familiar people**
- **Pain that breaks through medication**, or a vet telling you doses are maxed out

Any one of these deserves a call to your vet today rather than next week. Several together usually means the window has arrived.

Two things that are _not_ reliable signals on their own: age, and a scary diagnosis. A 14-year-old dog who eats, walks, and wags is not a candidate. A cancer diagnosis is a starting point for a conversation about time and comfort, not an automatic answer.

## What This Looks Like Across Different Dogs

**Large and giant breeds** often decline through mobility first. A Great Dane or Labrador who can no longer rise on a slick floor loses hygiene and happiness quickly, even with pain well controlled. Rugs, a harness with a handle, and a raised bed can genuinely buy good months — try those before concluding it is time.

**Small breeds** more often decline through organ disease and dental pain, which are easier to miss. A Chihuahua who stops eating may be in mouth pain, not at the end of life. Rule that out first.

**Cognitive decline** is its own category. A dog with canine cognitive dysfunction may be physically fine but awake and distressed all night. Sundowning that no longer responds to medication is a legitimate quality-of-life failure, even with a perfect mobility score.

If a specific condition is driving the decline, veterinary hospice is worth asking about. The [AKC's guide to palliative and hospice care](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/palliative-care-and-hospice-for-terminally-ill-dogs/) covers what that support actually involves — it is a real middle path, not a delay tactic.

## Practical Takeaways

- **Score the HHHHHMM scale twice, a week apart.** The direction of travel is your answer.
- **Keep a good-day / bad-day calendar.** Two weeks of marks beats months of impressions.
- **Ask your vet the direct question**: "If this were your dog, what would you do, and when?" Most vets will answer honestly when asked that way.
- **Separate your dog's suffering from your own grief.** The AKC puts this plainly — the decision has to be about easing their suffering, not postponing yours.
- **Decide what a good last day looks like** before you need it: favorite food, a patch of sun, the people they love, at home if you can arrange it.

One last thing worth saying clearly: choosing euthanasia at the right moment is not a failure of care. It is the last piece of care you get to give. Dogs do not fear death the way we do — they fear pain and confusion, and you have the power to spare them both.
