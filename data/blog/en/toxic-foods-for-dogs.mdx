## The Short Answer

Most of what's on your plate is fine to share in moderation — but a handful of common foods are genuinely dangerous for dogs, and a few can cause serious harm in surprisingly small amounts. According to the [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/people-foods-avoid-feeding-your-pets), the foods to keep away from dogs include chocolate, xylitol, grapes and raisins, onions and garlic, macadamia nuts, alcohol, caffeine, and avocado. Below is what each one does, and the signs to watch for.

## The Foods That Cause the Most Trouble

These are the ones worth memorizing. They send the most dogs to the emergency vet.

### Chocolate

Chocolate contains methylxanthines (caffeine and theobromine), which dogs process slowly. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/people-foods-avoid-feeding-your-pets) notes it can cause vomiting, diarrhea, panting, excessive thirst and urination, an abnormal heart rhythm, tremors, and seizures. **Darker and more bitter chocolate is more dangerous** — baking chocolate and cocoa powder are the worst offenders, milk chocolate less so, but none of it belongs in a dog.

### Grapes and Raisins

These are unpredictable and genuinely risky. Even small amounts can cause kidney failure in some dogs, and there's no reliable "safe" dose. Per [VCA Animal Hospitals](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/grape-raisin-and-currant-poisoning-in-dogs), the earliest sign is usually stomach upset within 12 to 24 hours — vomiting, loss of appetite, diarrhea, and lethargy — with more severe signs appearing over the next day or two. This one is a call-your-vet-now food.

### Xylitol

Xylitol is a sugar substitute hiding in sugar-free gum, mints, peanut butter, baked goods, and some medications. In dogs it triggers a rapid drop in blood sugar and can cause liver damage. Early signs include vomiting, weakness, and loss of coordination that can progress to seizures. Because it's so common in "sugar-free" products, always check the label before sharing anything sweet.

### Onions and Garlic

Onions, garlic, leeks, and chives all damage a dog's red blood cells, which can lead to anemia. This includes cooked, raw, and powdered forms — so seasoned leftovers, onion soup mix, and garlic bread all count. The effect is dose-dependent and can build up over repeated small exposures.

### Macadamia Nuts

Macadamia nuts can cause weakness, vomiting, tremors, and a fever-like reaction, usually within 12 hours. We cover this one in depth in our guide to [whether dogs can eat macadamia nuts](/blog/can-dogs-eat-macadamia-nuts) — the short version is: no.

### Alcohol, Caffeine, and Raw Dough

Alcohol (including foods soaked in it) and caffeine are both dangerous, and raw yeast dough is a double threat — it expands in the warm stomach and ferments into alcohol. Keep coffee grounds, tea bags, and rising dough well out of reach.

## Signs of Food Poisoning to Watch For

Symptoms vary by food, but these are the ones that should put you on alert after your dog eats something they shouldn't have:

- Vomiting or diarrhea
- Drooling, or repeated lip-licking and swallowing
- Weakness, lethargy, or wobbly, uncoordinated movement
- Panting, restlessness, or trembling
- Excessive thirst or urination
- Loss of appetite

Timing matters. Chocolate and xylitol can produce signs within an hour or two; grape toxicity may take most of a day to show up. **Absence of symptoms right away doesn't mean your dog is in the clear.**

## When to Call Your Vet

Don't wait it out. If you know or suspect your dog ate something toxic, call your veterinarian right away, or reach the [ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control) at **(888) 426-4435**, which runs 24 hours a day. A quick phone call is almost always the right move.

Before you call, try to note three things: what your dog ate, roughly how much, and when. That information helps the vet judge the risk fast. And unless a professional tells you to, **don't try to make your dog vomit at home** — with some substances that does more damage than the food itself.

## Building a Safer Kitchen

Most poisonings are accidents of opportunity: a bag left on the counter, a dropped square of chocolate, a purse with sugar-free gum inside. A few habits go a long way — keep counters clear, take out the trash promptly, and make sure everyone in the house knows the danger list.

It also helps to know what *is* safe, so you're not guessing every time. Our roundup of [human foods dogs can eat](/blog/what-human-food-can-dogs-eat) covers the green-light list, and if your dog gets into non-food trouble, our guide to [household items that are dangerous to dogs](/blog/household-items-dangerous-to-dogs) is worth a look too.

## Practical Takeaways

- **Memorize the big six:** chocolate, grapes/raisins, xylitol, onions/garlic, macadamia nuts, and alcohol.
- **Small amounts can still matter** — especially grapes and xylitol, where there's no reliable safe dose.
- **Watch for vomiting, weakness, wobbliness, and tremors** after any suspected exposure.
- **Save the number:** ASPCA Animal Poison Control, (888) 426-4435.
- **Don't induce vomiting** unless your vet or poison control tells you to.
