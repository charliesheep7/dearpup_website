## Why Your Training Method Matters

There are dozens of training styles out there, and every trainer seems to swear by a different one. **The good news: the research points in one clear direction.** Reward-based training gets results and keeps your dog happy while doing it.

The [American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior (AVSAB)](https://avsab.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/AVSAB-Humane-Dog-Training-Position-Statement-2021.pdf) puts it plainly in its position statement: evidence supports reward-based methods for all canine training. It also states there is no evidence that aversive training is necessary.

Your method shapes more than whether your dog learns "sit." It shapes how your dog feels about you, about new situations, and about learning itself.

## What to Look For in Any Method

Before comparing styles, here's what separates good training from frustrating training:

- **Clear communication.** Your dog should know exactly which action earned the reward.
- **Good timing.** Rewards need to land within a second or two of the behavior.
- **Small steps.** Break big skills into tiny, easy wins.
- **Consistency.** Everyone in the house uses the same cues and rules.
- **A dog who wants to participate.** If your dog is avoiding sessions, something needs to change.

## Positive Reinforcement (Reward-Based Training)

**How it works:** You reward the behavior you want with something your dog values: food, play, praise, or access to something fun. Behavior that pays gets repeated.

Unwanted behavior isn't ignored forever. Instead, you manage the setting so it's harder to practice, and you teach an alternative. A dog who jumps on guests learns that sitting gets the attention instead.

**Pros:**

- Backed by the most research for both results and welfare
- Builds trust and a dog who is eager to work
- Safe for puppies, seniors, and anxious dogs

**Cons:**

- Takes patience and good timing at first
- You need to find rewards your dog truly cares about

## Clicker (Marker) Training

**How it works:** Clicker training is a type of positive reinforcement. You use a distinct sound to "mark" the exact moment your dog does the right thing, then deliver a treat.

According to the [AKC](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/training/clicker-training-your-dog-mark-and-reward/), you first "load" the clicker by clicking and treating 10 to 20 times until your dog learns the sound predicts a reward. Then you click at the precise moment, such as when your dog's rear touches the ground for a sit. Once the behavior is learned, the clicker can be phased out.

**Pros:**

- Extremely precise, which speeds up learning
- Great for tricks, shaping complex behaviors, and distance work

**Cons:**

- One more thing to hold during walks
- A verbal marker like "yes" works nearly as well for many owners

## Luring, Capturing, and Shaping

These are three ways to get the behavior in the first place, and all fit inside reward-based training.

- **Luring:** Guide your dog into position with a treat in your hand. Fast for basics like sit and down. Fade the lure quickly so your dog doesn't wait to see food.
- **Capturing:** Wait for your dog to do something naturally, like lying down, then mark and reward it.
- **Shaping:** Reward small steps toward a bigger goal. Useful for complex behaviors like going to a mat and settling.

Our step-by-step guide on [how to train your dog](/blog/how-to-train-your-dog) shows how to use luring for the first six cues.

## Balanced Training and Aversive Methods

**How it works:** Balanced training mixes rewards with corrections, such as leash jerks, prong collars, or e-collars. Purely aversive training relies mainly on physical or emotional discomfort to stop behavior.

The research here is hard to ignore. A [2020 study in _PLOS ONE_](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7743949/) followed 92 companion dogs from reward-based and aversive-based training schools. Dogs from the aversive schools showed more stress behaviors during training and larger post-training increases in the stress hormone cortisol. They were also more "pessimistic" in a later thinking test.

AVSAB also cites research where obedience was highest in dogs trained only with rewards and lowest in dogs trained only with aversive methods. Mixed or "balanced" training landed in between.

**Pros:**

- Can suppress a behavior quickly in the moment

**Cons:**

- Linked to more stress, fear, and anxiety-related behavior
- Can damage the relationship between you and your dog
- AVSAB links aversive methods to a higher risk of aggression and anxiety problems

If your dog already struggles with fear or stress, our guide to [dog anxiety](/blog/dog-anxiety) explains why a gentle approach matters even more.

## Dominance or "Alpha" Training

This approach is built on the idea that you need to be the "pack leader." It often includes techniques like "alpha rolls," where a dog is pinned on its back.

AVSAB specifically lists alpha rolls and "dominance downs" among techniques to avoid. Modern behavior science doesn't support treating everyday problems like pulling or jumping as a fight for rank. Most of those behaviors simply haven't been taught another way yet.

## How to Choose the Right Method for Your Dog

**Our recommendation: start with positive reinforcement and add a marker (clicker or "yes") for precision.** It's the method with the strongest evidence, and it works for almost every dog.

Then adjust for your dog:

- **Puppies:** Keep sessions to a few minutes and use lots of luring. Our [puppy training tips](/blog/puppy-training-tips) cover the first months.
- **Food-motivated dogs:** Treats make everything easier. Use small, soft pieces.
- **Toy-driven dogs:** Use a quick game of tug or fetch as the reward. See our ideas for [games for dogs](/blog/games-for-dogs).
- **Anxious or reactive dogs:** Work well below the level that triggers fear, and talk to your vet about extra support.

### Choosing a trainer

Ask any trainer what happens when a dog gets something wrong. A good answer focuses on changing the setup and rewarding the right choice. If the answer involves pain, fear, or "showing the dog who's boss," keep looking.

## Practical Takeaways

- Reward-based training has the strongest evidence for results and welfare
- Clicker training is a precise version of positive reinforcement, not a separate philosophy
- Luring, capturing, and shaping are tools for getting a behavior to reward
- Aversive and "balanced" methods are linked to more stress and lower obedience
- Short, consistent daily sessions matter more than any single technique
