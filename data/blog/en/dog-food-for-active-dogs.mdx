## The Short Answer

An active dog needs **more calories, not a completely different kind of food**. A complete and balanced diet is still the foundation. What changes is how much energy it delivers and how much of it your dog eats.

If your dog runs, hikes, swims, or does agility several times a week, the right food is one that is nutritionally complete, calorie-dense enough to keep weight steady, and easy to digest. Here's how to judge that on a label.

## Why Active Dogs Eat Differently

Energy has to come from somewhere. Per the [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/dog-care/dog-nutrition-tips), fats are the most concentrated form of food energy, providing more than twice the energy of proteins or carbohydrates.

That's why foods marketed for performance or high-energy dogs usually lean on fat and protein. Carbohydrates still have a place. [VCA Animal Hospitals](https://www.vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/nutrition-general-feeding-guidelines-for-dogs) notes they are a readily available energy source for dogs, and including them can reduce the amount of animal protein and fat needed to meet energy needs.

Fiber is where active dogs differ. The ASPCA points out that foods high in fiber are not a good fit for dogs with high energy requirements, so a lower-fiber, more calorie-dense food is usually the better fit.

## What to Look For on the Label

Start with the basics, then look at energy.

1. **A "complete and balanced" statement** for your dog's life stage. Without it, no amount of extra calories fixes the gaps.
2. **A named protein source** near the top of the ingredient list.
3. **A moderate-to-higher fat level**, which is the easiest way to add calories without adding bulk.
4. **Moderate fiber.** Enough to keep stools healthy, not so much that your dog fills up before getting enough energy.
5. **Calories per cup** printed on the bag. Two foods can look identical and differ a lot here.

A food that checks those boxes is usually a better pick than one with a trendy ingredient and no calorie information. If you want a broader framework for comparing foods, our guide on [what dog food is better](/blog/what-dog-food-is-better) walks through it.

## How Much Should an Active Dog Eat?

Start with the feeding guide on the bag, then adjust. VCA says package guidelines can be used as a starting point for a healthy dog, and that your vet can run an energy calculation based on weight, activity, and other factors for a more individualized number of daily calories.

That second part matters for active dogs, because package charts often assume average activity. If you want to see how the math works, try our post on the [RER calculator for dogs](/blog/rer-calculator-dog), and see [how much food to feed your dog](/blog/how-much-food-should-i-feed-my-dog) for the everyday basics.

Then let your dog tell you:

- **Weight dropping or ribs very prominent?** Increase the portion a little, or move to a more calorie-dense food.
- **Weight creeping up?** Trim back. Active seasons end, and calorie needs drop with them.
- **Energy flat?** Check with your vet before changing food. It may not be a food problem.

Make changes gradually and re-check every couple of weeks.

## Meal Timing and Water

VCA says feeding once or twice a day works for most pet dogs, and that many dogs benefit from equally divided meals two to three times per day. For a dog that exercises hard, smaller meals are a sensible way to go.

Water matters just as much as food. The ASPCA notes that a 10% decrease in body water can cause serious illness, so bring water on every hike, run, or beach day, and offer it often.

## Active Puppies Are a Special Case

A puppy that never stops moving is also growing, and that changes the plan. VCA notes puppies have a high energy requirement in their first few months because they grow so quickly, and with small stomachs they usually need several small meals a day rather than one or two big ones.

Choose a food labeled for growth, follow the label's puppy chart, and ask your vet before switching to an adult or performance formula. Our guide on [how much to feed your dog](/blog/how-much-food-should-i-feed-my-dog) covers the everyday basics.

## Do Active Dogs Need Supplements?

Usually not. The ASPCA says that with a complete and balanced diet, vitamin supplements are unnecessary unless a vet diagnoses a specific deficiency, and that poisoning from excess vitamins is now more common than deficiency. Check with your vet before adding anything, especially joint or performance products.

## Practical Takeaways

- Pick a complete and balanced food, then adjust calories for activity.
- Fat is the efficient energy source; avoid very high-fiber formulas for hard-working dogs.
- Use the package amount as a starting point and let body condition guide changes.
- Ask your vet for a calorie target if your dog's activity level is well above average.
- Keep water close, especially during exercise.
