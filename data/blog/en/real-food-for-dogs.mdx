## What Is "Real Food" for Dogs? The Short Answer

**"Real food" for dogs usually means meals made from whole, recognizable ingredients like meat, eggs, vegetables, and grains, instead of highly processed kibble.** It covers fresh-cooked delivery diets, home-cooked meals, and whole-food toppers added to regular dog food.

It is an appealing idea, and it can be a great way to feed. But "real" is a marketing word, not a nutrition standard. A bowl of chicken and rice looks wholesome and can still be missing calcium, vitamins, and essential fatty acids your dog needs every day.

Here is how to get the benefits of real food without the pitfalls.

## The Three Ways People Feed Real Food

### 1. Commercial fresh food

These are gently cooked, refrigerated or frozen meals, often delivered to your door. [PetMD describes fresh dog food](https://www.petmd.com/dog/nutrition/pros-and-cons-fresh-dog-food) as minimally processed, made without preservatives, and made with human-grade ingredients like lean meats, vegetables, and whole grains.

The catch is cost. PetMD estimates **fresh dog food at about $1.40 per 100 calories, compared with about 25 cents for dry food** and about $1.10 for canned. For a large dog, that adds up quickly. PetMD also notes that not every fresh brand adds a full vitamin and mineral blend, so check the label for a "complete and balanced" statement. Our overview of [fresh dog food](/blog/fresh-dog-food) compares the options.

### 2. Home-cooked meals

Cooking for your dog gives you full control of ingredients. It also puts the job of balancing nutrients on you, and that is harder than it looks.

In what researchers called the largest evaluation of its kind, a [UC Davis study](https://www.ucdavis.edu/news/homemade-dog-food-recipes-can-be-risky-business-study-finds) published in _JAVMA_ analyzed 200 home-prepared dog food recipes. **Very few provided all essential nutrients in adequate amounts**, including recipes written by veterinarians. Our guide to [homemade dog kibble](/blog/homemade-dog-kibble) covers how to do it more safely.

### 3. Real-food toppers

This is the easiest and cheapest option. You keep a complete and balanced base food and add a spoonful of whole food on top, like cooked chicken, an egg, or steamed green beans. The base does the nutritional heavy lifting, and the topper adds variety and flavor.

## Is Real Food Actually Better?

The honest answer: it depends on the specific diet, not the category.

**Possible benefits:**

- Many dogs find fresh food very tasty, which helps picky eaters
- Higher moisture content supports hydration
- Some owners notice firmer stools or a shinier coat
- Familiar ingredients make it easier to see what your dog is eating

**Possible downsides:**

- Cost, often several times more than kibble
- Home recipes are frequently unbalanced
- Fresh and raw foods spoil faster and need careful handling
- Less processed does not always mean more nutritious

Long-term research directly comparing fresh diets to good-quality kibble is still limited. What we do know is that **a complete and balanced diet at the right calorie level** is one of the biggest drivers of a long, healthy life. Keeping your dog lean matters more than whether their food comes in a bag or a box.

## What About Raw "Real Food"?

Raw diets are often marketed as the most natural option. The [AVMA discourages feeding raw or undercooked animal protein](https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/pet-owners/petcare/raw-pet-foods-and-avma-policy) to pets because of the risk of bacteria like _Salmonella_ and _Listeria_, which can make both dogs and people sick. If you want real food, lightly cooked is the safer path. See our guide to [raw dog food brands](/blog/raw-dog-food-brands) if you are weighing the pros and cons.

## How to Add Real Food Safely

1. **Keep a balanced base.** Look for a complete and balanced statement on the label. The [WSAVA's guide to selecting a pet food](https://wsava.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Selecting-a-pet-food-for-your-pet-updated-2021_WSAVA-Global-Nutrition-Toolkit.pdf) lists smart questions to ask any brand.
2. **Keep extras small.** A common vet rule of thumb is to keep treats and add-ins to about 10% of daily calories. Our [dog calorie calculator](/blog/dog-calorie-calculator) helps you find that number.
3. **Cook plain.** No butter, oil, salt, onion, or garlic.
4. **Know the no-go list.** The [ASPCA's list of people foods to avoid](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/people-foods-avoid-feeding-your-pets) includes grapes, raisins, chocolate, xylitol, onions, and garlic.
5. **Introduce new foods slowly.** Sudden changes can upset your dog's stomach. Our guide to [changing dog food](/blog/changing-dog-food) walks through a gradual switch.

### Real foods most dogs can enjoy

- Plain cooked chicken, turkey, or lean beef
- Cooked eggs (see [can dogs eat eggs](/blog/can-dogs-eat-eggs))
- Plain cooked rice or oats
- Green beans, carrots, and pumpkin
- Blueberries and apple slices without seeds

For a longer list, see our guide to [what human food dogs can eat](/blog/what-human-food-can-dogs-eat).

## Practical Takeaways

- "Real food" is a marketing term, so judge a diet by balance, not by looks
- Fresh commercial diets can be excellent but cost much more than kibble
- Most homemade recipes are unbalanced, so use one from a veterinary nutritionist
- Toppers are the easiest way to add real food to a balanced base
- Keep extras to about 10% of calories and skip toxic foods
