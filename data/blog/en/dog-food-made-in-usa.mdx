## The Short Answer

When a bag of dog food says **Made in USA** with no extra wording, it is making a strong legal promise. Under the [Federal Trade Commission standard](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-made-usa-standard), the product must be "all or virtually all" made in the United States. That covers where it is processed and where its ingredients come from.

Plenty of bags use softer wording, though. "Made in the USA with globally sourced ingredients" or "Manufactured in the USA" can mean something quite different. Knowing the difference takes about a minute once you know what to look for.

## What "All or Virtually All" Means

The FTC breaks its standard into three parts. For an unqualified Made in USA claim:

- **Final assembly or processing** happens in the United States
- **All significant processing** that goes into the product happens in the United States
- **All or virtually all ingredients or components** are made and sourced in the United States

In plain terms, the product should have no foreign content, or only a negligible amount. A dog food cooked and bagged in Ohio, but built around imported lamb, does not meet that bar.

For pet food, [Hill's Pet Nutrition](https://www.hillspet.com/dog-care/nutrition-feeding/dog-food-made-in-the-usa) quotes the ASPCA's Mindy Bough explaining that this includes the packaging, the ingredients and the production of the food.

### The 2021 labeling rule

In August 2021 the FTC finalized its Made in USA Labeling Rule. It writes the "all or virtually all" standard into law for product labels with unqualified claims. It also covers online and mail-order materials that show a Made in USA seal, mark, tag or stamp. Brands that break the rule can face civil penalties.

## Qualified Claims: The Fine Print Worth Reading

A **qualified** claim tells you how much of a product is domestic. The FTC describes it as a claim that explains "the extent, amount or type" of domestic content or processing. It signals that the product is not entirely American.

Examples you might see on a dog food bag:

- "Made in the USA with lamb from New Zealand" (the example Hill's and PetMD both use)
- "Made in the USA from domestic and imported ingredients"
- "Manufactured in the USA" or "Cooked in the USA"
- A percentage, like the FTC's own example of "60% U.S. content"

None of these is a red flag on its own. Some ingredients are simply easier to source well from abroad. The point is that a qualified claim is honest about the mix, and you can decide what matters to you.

### "Manufactured in" is not "sourced in"

This is the gap most shoppers miss. A phrase like "manufactured in the USA" describes where the food was mixed, cooked and packaged. It does not promise anything about where the chicken, fish or grains came from. If ingredient origin matters to you, look for brands that say where their main proteins are sourced, or ask them directly.

## Does Made in USA Mean Better Food?

Not by itself. Country of origin tells you about sourcing and manufacturing. It does not tell you whether a food suits your dog's age, size or health.

A US-made food can still be a poor match for a senior dog with kidney concerns or a puppy with high energy needs. An imported food can be complete, balanced and well made. When you compare foods, origin is one useful filter among several:

- **Nutritional adequacy statement.** Look for wording that the food is complete and balanced for your dog's life stage.
- **Ingredient list.** Our guide to [dog food ingredients](/blog/dog-food-ingredients) explains what the common names mean.
- **Your dog's needs.** Activity, age and any health conditions should drive the choice. See our [healthy dog diet](/blog/healthy-dog-diet) basics.
- **Budget.** A good food you can afford every month beats a premium one you can't. Our list of [affordable healthy dog food](/blog/affordable-healthy-dog-food) can help.

The Association of American Feed Control Officials (AAFCO) also sets rules for how named ingredients appear on US pet food labels, according to Hill's. That's a separate question from where the food is made, but it's worth understanding both.

## How to Check Where Your Dog Food Is Made

You don't need to be a label detective. A quick routine works for most bags:

1. **Read the exact wording.** Is it a plain "Made in USA," or does it add "with," "from" or "globally sourced"? The extra words are the qualifier.
2. **Find the manufacturer details.** The bag should list who made or distributed the food. That gives you someone to contact.
3. **Check the brand's website.** Many brands publish where they source key proteins and where their kitchens are.
4. **Ask questions.** PetMD suggests asking manufacturers who formulates their diets, what credentials that person has and where the food is produced.
5. **Talk to your vet.** They can tell you whether a food fits your dog, wherever it was made.

If you think a food has made your dog sick, PetMD advises stopping that food right away and contacting both the manufacturer and your vet. Hill's notes that the FTC needs a complaint before it can look into a possible problem with a food label.

## Types of US-Made Food to Compare

US manufacturing shows up across every style of dog food:

- **Kibble.** Shelf-stable and budget-friendly. Our [kibble dog food](/blog/kibble-dog-food) guide covers what to look for.
- **Fresh or gently cooked food.** Often made in smaller batches and shipped chilled. See our [fresh dog food](/blog/fresh-dog-food) overview.
- **Raw food.** Some owners prefer it, but it carries extra handling risks. Our roundup of [raw dog food brands](/blog/raw-dog-food-brands) covers the trade-offs.

Whatever the format, switch foods gradually over several days to avoid an upset stomach.

## Practical Takeaways

- A plain "Made in USA" claim means all or virtually all US ingredients and processing.
- Extra wording like "with imported ingredients" is a qualified claim, and it's allowed.
- "Manufactured in the USA" describes the kitchen, not the ingredient source.
- Origin is one filter. Nutritional adequacy and your dog's needs matter more.
- When in doubt, ask the brand and your vet.
