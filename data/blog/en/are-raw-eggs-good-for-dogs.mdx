## Are Raw Eggs Good for Dogs? The Short Answer

**No — and the reason is refreshingly simple. Raw eggs are not more nutritious than cooked ones, they just carry more risk.**

[PetMD](https://www.petmd.com/dog/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-eggs) puts it plainly: "dogs should not eat raw eggs. Although raw eggs contain the same nutrients as cooked eggs, they come with risks and offer no additional health benefits."

That is the whole argument in one sentence. There is no nutritional upside you give up by reaching for a pan. Cooking costs you two minutes and removes both of the problems below.

## The Two Real Problems With Raw

### Salmonella

Raw eggs can carry bacteria, and dogs are not immune to it. The [American Kennel Club](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-eggs/) notes that **dogs "may be at risk of coming into contact with salmonella bacteria when eating raw eggs or other raw foods."** Salmonellosis can bring fever, vomiting, diarrhea, and lethargy.

There is a second angle owners forget: you handle the food too. When the [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/get-facts-raw-pet-food-diets-can-be-dangerous-you-and-your-pet) tested 196 raw pet food samples, **15 were positive for Salmonella and 32 for Listeria monocytogenes** — while dry food, semi-moist food, and jerky treats came back with zero or minimal contamination. The FDA warns that people feeding raw have "a higher risk of getting infected," and that bacteria travel easily to other people, objects, and surfaces.

Raw eggs are not raw meat, but the handling principle is the same: what goes in the bowl ends up on your hands, your counter, and your dog's face.

### Avidin and Biotin

This one is specific to eggs. The AKC explains that **"feeding raw eggs can lead to biotin deficiency, as egg whites contain avidin, an enzyme that prevents the absorption of biotin in the body."** Biotin supports skin, coat, and metabolism — exactly the things people feed raw eggs hoping to improve.

PetMD confirms the fix: "Cooking eggs neutralizes avidin, making them a safer and more nutritious choice for dogs."

So the popular "raw eggs for a shiny coat" advice quietly works against itself. The nutrients are real; the raw part is what gets in the way.

## What Eggs Actually Give Your Dog

Cooked eggs are genuinely good food. According to the AKC, they supply **protein, healthy fats, iron, folate, riboflavin, selenium, vitamin A, and vitamin B12** — a useful combination for skin health and a shiny coat.

They are also cheap, easy, and something almost every dog will happily eat. As a topper for a picky eater or an occasional treat, a plain cooked egg is hard to beat.

## How Much Egg, and How to Cook It

PetMD's preparation advice is short: **"The best way to prepare eggs for your dogs is by cooking them thoroughly with no added salt, butter, or oil."** Plain scrambled or hard-boiled are both fine. Skip the seasoning entirely.

On portion, PetMD recommends roughly:

- **Extra-small dogs (2–10 lbs)**: about a quarter of an egg
- **Extra-large dogs (91+ lbs)**: up to two eggs
- **Frequency**: one to two times per week

The guiding rule underneath all of it: **"Eggs should be considered a treat, and all treats combined should make up no more than 10% of your dog's daily calories."** Eggs are calorie-dense, and a dog already eating a complete food does not need them daily.

## What If My Dog Already Ate a Raw Egg?

This happens, usually involving a dropped egg and a fast dog. Most healthy dogs are fine.

Watch for vomiting, diarrhea, fever, or unusual lethargy over the next day or two, and call your vet if any of those show up or persist. The biotin issue is a long-term, repeated-feeding concern — not something a single egg causes.

The one group worth extra care with: puppies, senior dogs, and dogs with compromised immune systems, who handle a bacterial hit less easily than a healthy adult.

## What About the Shell?

Raw-feeding advice often includes the shell, on the logic that it adds calcium. The calcium part is true — eggshell is largely calcium carbonate — but it does not rescue the case for raw.

A shell from a raw egg carries the same surface bacteria concern as the egg inside it, and sharp shell fragments are their own small hazard. A dog on a complete commercial food is also already getting balanced calcium, and adding more is not automatically better. Calcium and phosphorus need to stay in balance, which is exactly what a complete diet is formulated to do.

If you want to use shells anyway, talk to your vet first, and cook and grind them rather than serving them raw and cracked. For a fuller look, see [can dogs eat egg shells](/blog/can-dogs-eat-egg-shells).

## Practical Takeaways

- Raw eggs offer **no nutritional advantage** over cooked ones.
- Raw carries a salmonella risk for your dog and for you.
- Avidin in raw whites blocks biotin; cooking neutralizes it.
- Cook plain, with no salt, butter, or oil.
- Keep eggs to a treat — under 10% of daily calories, one to two times a week.

If you like giving your dog eggs, keep doing it. Just cook them first. You lose nothing and skip both problems entirely.
