You catch your dog nosing around the lilac bush, or chewing a fallen flower, and now you're searching for answers. The good news is that the classic garden lilac isn't the plant to worry about most.

The confusing part is the name. Two very different plants are called "lilac," and only one of them appears on the ASPCA's list of plants toxic to dogs.

## The Short Answer

**True lilacs (Syringa) are not listed as toxic to dogs, but Persian lilac (Melia azedarach) is.** They're unrelated plants that happen to share a name.

- **True lilac:** the fragrant garden shrub most people picture. It isn't named on the [ASPCA dog plant list](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/dogs-plant-list).
- **Persian lilac:** also called chinaberry tree, bead tree, or Pride-of-India. The [ASPCA lists it as toxic](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/bead-tree) to dogs, cats, and horses.

If you can't tell which plant your dog got into, play it safe and call your vet.

## Why the Two Lilacs Are So Different

### True lilac (Syringa)

Being absent from the ASPCA list is reassuring, but it isn't a guarantee. The ASPCA says its list isn't meant to be all-inclusive, and that eating any plant material may cause vomiting and stomach upset in pets.

[Cooperative Extension](https://horses.extension.org/are-lilac-bushes-toxic-to-horses/) says lilac bushes aren't considered toxic to livestock, though it still advises keeping horses away from them and not feeding clippings. That's a sensible approach for dogs too: not a crisis if they nibble one, but not a snack either.

So a dog who eats a few true lilac blooms may be fine or may have a mild upset stomach. A dog who eats a lot of any plant deserves a call to the vet.

### Persian lilac (Melia azedarach)

This is the one to take seriously. Despite the name, Extension notes that Persian lilac isn't related to true lilacs. It's a tree in the Meliaceae family, and its toxins are called meliatoxins.

According to the ASPCA, **the ripe berries are the most toxic part**, but the bark, leaves, and flowers are toxic too. That matters in the yard, because fallen berries sit right at dog height.

## How to Tell Which Plant You Have

If you're not sure what's growing in your yard, check the plant label or ask a local nursery. A clear photo of the leaves, flowers, and any berries helps. Your vet or a poison hotline can also use the scientific name if you have it.

Here's the quick comparison:

| | True lilac | Persian lilac |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scientific name | Syringa | Melia azedarach |
| Other names | Common lilac | Chinaberry, bead tree, Pride-of-India |
| ASPCA dog plant list | Not listed | Toxic |
| Main concern | Possible stomach upset | Berries, bark, leaves, and flowers |

Because plant names get muddled so easily, the ASPCA suggests checking a plant's name to confirm its toxicity, since individual plants can look different from photos. When you're buying something new, look for the scientific name on the tag.

## Signs to Watch For

The ASPCA lists these clinical signs for Persian lilac:

- Vomiting
- Diarrhea
- Salivation
- Depression
- Weakness
- Seizures

Mild stomach upset can follow any plant snack. Weakness, seizures, or repeated vomiting are different. Those need a vet right away, whichever plant was involved.

## When to Call Your Vet

Call your vet or a poison hotline if:

- Your dog ate any part of a Persian lilac or chinaberry tree, even if they seem fine
- You can't identify the plant
- Your dog ate a large amount of any plant
- Your dog has any of the signs above

[ASPCA Poison Control](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/dogs-plant-list) is at (888) 426-4435. Take a photo of the plant, bring a sample if you can, and note roughly how much your dog ate and when. Your vet will take it from there.

## Making Your Yard Dog-Friendly

You don't need to tear out your lilac bush. A few small habits make a big difference:

- **Know what you've planted.** Keep plant tags or a simple list of what's in the yard.
- **Pick up fallen berries and clippings.** This matters most if you have a chinaberry tree nearby.
- **Watch puppies closely.** Curious chewers will try anything once.
- **Choose dog-friendly plants for new beds.** Our list of [flowers safe for dogs](/blog/flowers-safe-for-dogs) is a good place to start.
