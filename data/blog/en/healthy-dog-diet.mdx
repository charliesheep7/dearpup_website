Walk down any pet food aisle and you will see words like "natural," "ancestral," "holistic," and "human-grade" stacked on every bag. None of them tell you whether the food is actually good for your dog. **A healthy canine diet comes down to two things: balance and amount.** Get those right and the rest is mostly preference.

Here is what that looks like in practice.

## What Is a Healthy Canine Diet? The Short Answer

A healthy dog diet gives your dog every essential nutrient in the right proportions for their age and size, in an amount that keeps them at a lean body weight. That is it.

It does not have to be expensive, raw, grain-free, or homemade. Plenty of dogs live long, healthy lives on a solid commercial kibble. Plenty of dogs on pricey boutique diets end up overweight or short on key nutrients. The label on the front of the bag matters far less than the small print on the back.

## The Six Nutrients Every Dog Needs

According to [PetMD’s guide to dog food nutrients](https://www.petmd.com/dog/nutrition/evr_dg_whats_in_a_balanced_dog_food), a balanced dog diet includes six building blocks:

- **Protein** — builds and repairs muscle, skin, coat, and organs. Dogs need a steady supply of amino acids from quality protein sources like chicken, beef, fish, or eggs. Our guide to [protein for dogs](/blog/protein-for-dogs) goes deeper on sources.
- **Fat** — the most concentrated source of energy, plus the fatty acids that keep skin supple and the coat shiny.
- **Carbohydrates and fiber** — an energy source and, just as important, fiber for healthy digestion and firm stools.
- **Vitamins** — small amounts, big jobs: vision, immunity, blood clotting, and energy metabolism.
- **Minerals** — calcium and phosphorus for bones, plus trace minerals like zinc and iron.
- **Water** — the one most people forget. Fresh water should always be available.

The trick is not just having these nutrients but having them in the right ratios. Too little calcium can weaken bones; too much can cause skeletal problems in growing large-breed puppies. That balance is why "complete and balanced" is the most important phrase on any dog food.

## How to Tell If a Food Is Complete and Balanced

In the US, look for the nutritional adequacy statement on the bag. It is usually small text near the guaranteed analysis and reads something like: "Formulated to meet the nutritional levels established by the AAFCO Dog Food Nutrient Profiles for adult maintenance."

The [Association of American Feed Control Officials (AAFCO)](https://www.aafco.org/consumers/understanding-pet-food/selecting-the-right-pet-food/) sets these nutrient profiles, and the statement tells you three things:

1. **Whether the food is complete and balanced** — or only meant for "intermittent or supplemental feeding," like a topper or treat.
2. **Which life stage it is for** — growth (puppies), adult maintenance, or all life stages.
3. **How it was verified** — by formulation against the nutrient profile, or by an actual feeding trial.

As a benchmark, AAFCO’s minimum protein level is 18 percent on a dry-matter basis for adult maintenance and 22.5 percent for growth. Most quality foods sit comfortably above that.

The [World Small Animal Veterinary Association’s nutrition guidelines](https://wsava.org/global-guidelines/global-nutrition-guidelines/) add a few more questions worth asking a brand: Do they employ a qualified nutritionist? Who formulates their diets? What quality-control steps do they use? A company that answers clearly is usually a company that takes nutrition seriously.

If you are comparing specific foods, our breakdown of [what dog food is better](/blog/what-dog-food-is-better) walks through the trade-offs between formats.

## Portions: The Part Most Owners Get Wrong

Even the best food becomes an unhealthy diet if you feed too much of it. Extra weight is one of the most common — and most fixable — health problems in dogs.

The best evidence for this comes from a well-known lifetime study of Labrador Retrievers published in the Journal of the American Veterinary Medical Association in 2002. Dogs fed 25 percent less than their littermates stayed leaner and **lived a median of about 1.8 years longer**, with later onset of chronic conditions like arthritis.

A few practical rules:

- **Start with the bag’s feeding guide, then adjust.** Those numbers are a starting point for an average dog, not a prescription.
- **Use a measuring cup, not a scoop you eyeball.** Small overages add up fast.
- **Check body condition every few weeks.** You should be able to feel your dog’s ribs without pressing hard, and see a waist from above.
- **Account for treats.** Keep them under 10 percent of daily calories.

[VCA Animal Hospitals’ feeding guidelines](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/nutrition-general-feeding-guidelines-for-dogs) note that activity level, age, and whether a dog is spayed or neutered all shift calorie needs. ## Healthy Extras Worth Adding (and What to Skip)

A complete and balanced food already covers the essentials, so extras are a bonus, not a requirement. Good options in small amounts include:

- **Plain cooked lean meat** like chicken or turkey, unseasoned
- **Vegetables** such as green beans, carrots, or cooked pumpkin
- **Fruits** like blueberries, apple slices (no seeds), or banana
- **Plain cooked eggs** for a protein boost

Our list of [human foods dogs can eat](/blog/what-human-food-can-dogs-eat) covers more safe picks.

Some foods should never make it into the bowl. The [ASPCA’s list of people foods to avoid](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/people-foods-avoid-feeding-your-pets) includes chocolate, grapes and raisins, onions, garlic, xylitol, macadamia nuts, and alcohol. See our full rundown of [toxic foods for dogs](/blog/toxic-foods-for-dogs) before sharing anything new.

A note on homemade diets: cooking for your dog can work, but improvised recipes are very often short on calcium and micronutrients. If you want to go that route, use a recipe designed by a board-certified veterinary nutritionist.

## What This Means for Different Dogs

A healthy diet looks slightly different depending on who is eating it:

- **Puppies** need a growth or all-life-stages formula with more protein, fat, and carefully controlled calcium. Large-breed puppies should eat a food made specifically for large-breed growth.
- **Adult dogs** do best on an adult-maintenance food in portions that hold a lean body shape.
- **Senior dogs** often need fewer calories but just as much quality protein to hold on to muscle. Our guide to [senior dog food](/blog/senior-dog-food) covers the details.
- **Very active or working dogs** may need a higher-calorie, higher-fat performance diet.

If your dog has a medical condition — kidney disease, allergies, pancreatitis — ask your vet before changing anything. Therapeutic diets exist for a reason.

## Practical Takeaways

1. Pick a food with an AAFCO complete-and-balanced statement for your dog’s life stage.
2. Measure every meal and adjust portions to keep your dog lean.
3. Keep treats and toppers under 10 percent of daily calories.
4. Add small amounts of safe whole foods for variety if you like.
5. Switch foods gradually over 7 to 10 days to avoid stomach upset.
6. Check in with your vet about diet at every annual visit.

None of this is complicated. The dogs that do best are usually not the ones on the fanciest food — they are the ones whose owners feed a good, balanced diet consistently and keep them trim.
