Shopping for raw dog food is strange. Every brand's website looks the same, every one promises shinier coats and better digestion, and almost none of them lead with the one thing that separates a well-made raw food from a risky one.

So this guide skips the rankings. Brand lineups change constantly, and a list of names goes stale in a season. What does not go stale is the small set of checks that tells you whether any given raw brand is worth your money — and those checks take about ninety seconds per bag.

## Why Raw Food Needs a Different Shopping Checklist

Kibble is cooked, and cooking handles pathogens. Raw food, by definition, skips that step, which means the manufacturer has to control pathogens some other way — or not at all.

This is not a fringe concern. The [FDA notes](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/get-facts-raw-pet-food-diets-can-be-dangerous-you-and-your-pet) that raw pet food is more likely to carry _Salmonella_ and _Listeria monocytogenes_ than cooked food, and that **refrigeration and freezing do not kill those bacteria**. A frozen raw patty is a cold raw patty, not a sterilised one.

The [AVMA's policy](https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/avma-policies/raw-or-undercooked-animal-source-protein-cat-and-dog-diets) goes further and discourages feeding animal-source protein that has not been through a process to eliminate pathogens, partly because dogs can carry these organisms without looking sick and pass them to the people in the house.

None of that means every raw brand is dangerous. It means the gap between the careful brands and the careless ones is much wider in this category than in [kibble](/blog/kibble-dog-food) or [fresh cooked food](/blog/fresh-dog-food), and the label is where you find out which one you are holding.

## What to Look For: The Three Checks

### 1. A Named Pathogen-Control Step

The strongest signal a raw brand can give you is a specific, named process — most commonly **high-pressure processing (HPP)**, which uses cold water at very high pressure to inactivate pathogens without cooking the food. It is the same technique used on supermarket juices, guacamole, and baby food.

Some brands state it plainly on the package. Others bury it in an FAQ. A few skip pathogen control entirely and market "untouched, unprocessed" as a virtue — which is a real position some raw feeders hold, but you should know that is the trade you are making rather than discover it later.

If a brand cannot tell you what it does about pathogens, that is your answer.

### 2. An AAFCO Complete and Balanced Statement

This is the check people skip most often. A raw food is only a full diet if the label carries an **AAFCO statement saying it is complete and balanced** for a named life stage — adult maintenance, growth, or all life stages.

Plenty of raw products in the freezer aisle are ground meat-and-bone blends sold as toppers or components for a home-assembled diet. They are not wrong, but they are not dinner on their own, and a dog fed exclusively on an unbalanced blend can drift into real nutritional deficiencies over months.

Look for the life stage that matches your dog. Large-breed puppies in particular need a formula validated for large-breed growth, because calcium levels that are fine for an adult can cause skeletal problems in a growing Great Dane.

### 3. Handling Instructions You Can Actually Live With

Read the thaw and storage directions before you buy, not after. If a brand tells you to thaw in the fridge for 24 hours and use within 48, and your week does not have room for that, you will end up improvising — and improvised raw handling is where most household contamination happens.

The [VCA's overview of raw diets](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/dogs-and-raw-food-diets) is blunt about this: the risk to the humans in the house is a genuine part of the calculation, especially with young children, older adults, or anyone immunocompromised at home.

## Frozen Raw vs. Freeze-Dried Raw vs. DIY

Most of what people mean by "raw dog food brands" falls into three formats, and they fail in different ways.

| Format                  | Strengths                                                                       | Watch Out For                                                                    |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Frozen raw**          | Natural moisture, usually the lowest cost per calorie, widest range of formulas | Freezer space, thaw discipline, freezing alone is not pathogen control           |
| **Freeze-dried raw**    | Shelf-stable, easy to travel with and portion, no thawing                       | Higher cost per calorie, needs rehydration, "raw" still means raw                |
| **DIY / home-prepared** | Full control over ingredients and sourcing                                      | Very easy to get calcium, phosphorus, and trace minerals wrong without a formula |

Frozen and freeze-dried are both legitimate. The one that suits you is the one whose daily routine you will follow on a bad Tuesday, not the one that looks best on a Sunday.

DIY deserves one honest warning: home-prepared raw diets go out of balance easily, and the imbalances are invisible for a long time before they are not. If you want to go that route, work from a recipe formulated by a veterinary nutritionist rather than a forum post.

## How to Choose the Right One for Your Dog

Work through it in this order:

1. **Rule out on life stage first.** If the AAFCO statement does not cover your dog's stage, nothing else about the brand matters.
2. **Then rule out on pathogen control.** Decide whether you want a validated step like HPP. This is the single biggest safety lever you have, and it is a choice you make once.
3. **Then pick the format.** Frozen if you have freezer space and a steady routine; freeze-dried if you travel, have a small dog, or want zero thaw planning.
4. **Then compare price per day, not per bag.** Bag sizes and caloric density vary wildly across raw brands, so the sticker price tells you almost nothing.
5. **Then transition slowly.** Raw is a big change in moisture, fat, and protein. Give it a week or more — our guide to [changing dog food](/blog/changing-dog-food) walks through the pacing.

Two groups should talk to a vet before starting: households with an immunocompromised member, and dogs with pancreatitis, kidney disease, or a suppressed immune system. That is not a soft caution, it is the specific situation where raw goes wrong fastest.

## Practical Takeaways

- **Freezing is not a pathogen step.** If that is the brand's only answer, you are carrying the risk yourself.
- An **AAFCO complete-and-balanced statement** is the line between a diet and a topper. Check it every time the recipe changes.
- Brands reformulate. Re-read the label when the packaging design changes, not just when you switch brands.
- Wash bowls, utensils, and hands the way you would after handling raw chicken for yourself — because that is exactly what you are doing.
- Watch your own dog. Stool quality, energy, and coat tell you more over six weeks than any brand's marketing copy. If something is off, our guide to [food poisoning in dogs](/blog/canine-food-poisoning) covers what to watch for.

Raw feeding done carefully is a reasonable choice that plenty of owners make well. Raw feeding done casually is the version that ends up in FDA advisories. The difference is almost entirely in the three checks above — and in whether you are honest with yourself about the routine you will actually keep.
