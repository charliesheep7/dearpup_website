## The Short Answer

Yes, dogs can have shrimp — as long as it's **cooked, peeled, and completely plain**. Served that way, shrimp is a lean, protein-rich treat that most dogs happily inhale.

The catch is that the shrimp we make for ourselves rarely fits that description. Butter, garlic, lemon pepper, breading, and the shell all turn a safe snack into a problem. Cook a plain piece or two on the side, and you're good.

## Why Shrimp Is Good for Dogs

For such a small bite, shrimp carries a lot. According to [PetMD](https://www.petmd.com/dog/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-shrimp), it's a high-protein, low-calorie snack that supplies vitamin B12, niacin, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, iodine, antioxidants, and a little omega-3.

Those nutrients do real work:

- **Vitamin B12 and niacin** support metabolism and healthy energy production
- **Phosphorus** helps keep bones and teeth strong
- **Antioxidants** help fight the free radicals tied to aging
- **Omega-3s** support skin, coat, and joint health

The [AKC](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-shrimp/) notes shrimp is also naturally low in fat and carbohydrates, which makes it a reasonable treat even for dogs watching their weight. The one caveat: shrimp runs high in cholesterol and sodium, so it's an occasional snack — not a daily one.

## How Much Shrimp Can Your Dog Have?

Portion size scales with your dog's weight. PetMD's general guideline is a good starting point:

| Dog size | Weight | Safe amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Extra-small | 2–20 lbs | ½ medium shrimp |
| Small | 21–30 lbs | 1 medium shrimp |
| Medium | 31–50 lbs | 2 medium shrimp |
| Large | 51–90 lbs | 3 medium shrimp |
| Extra-large | 91+ lbs | up to 4 medium shrimp |

The bigger rule sits on top of that table: **treats of any kind should make up no more than 10% of your dog's daily calories.** The other 90% should come from a complete, balanced diet. If you're not sure how that 10% fits into your dog's day, our guide on [how much to feed your dog](/blog/how-much-food-should-i-feed-my-dog) breaks the math down.

## How to Serve Shrimp Safely

Getting shrimp right is mostly about prep. Four steps cover it:

1. **Cook it fully.** Boil, steam, or bake with nothing added. Heat kills the bacteria and parasites that make raw shrimp risky.
2. **Peel and devein.** Remove the shell, tail, legs, and the dark vein running along the back. Shells are a choking and blockage hazard; the vein is the shrimp's digestive tract.
3. **Skip every seasoning.** No butter, oil, salt, garlic, or onion. Garlic and onion are genuinely harmful to dogs, and the [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/people-foods-avoid-feeding-your-pets) lists both among people foods to keep away from pets.
4. **Cut it small.** Chop the cooked shrimp into bite-sized pieces so it's easy to chew and swallow — especially for small breeds.

The easiest habit: when shrimp is on the menu for you, set aside a plain piece or two and cook it before anything hits the pan. ## Allergies vs. Intolerance: What to Watch

There's a useful distinction here that trips a lot of owners up.

A **true shellfish allergy** is unlikely the first time your dog tries shrimp. Allergies develop from repeated exposure over time, so a brand-new food rarely triggers one on day one. When food allergies do form, the [VCA](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/food-allergies-in-dogs) notes they usually show up as itchy skin or chronic ear and digestive trouble.

A **food intolerance**, on the other hand, can happen on the very first bite. It's not an immune reaction — it's a digestive one — and it typically looks like vomiting or diarrhea a few hours after eating.

Because you can't predict which dog will react, the safe move is to **start with a single small piece and wait a day**. If your dog is fine, you can offer shrimp as an occasional treat within the portions above.

## When to Call Your Vet

Most shrimp mishaps are minor, but a few signs are worth a call. Reach out to your vet if you notice:

- Repeated vomiting or diarrhea that doesn't settle
- Blood in the vomit or stool
- Weakness, lethargy, or collapse
- Signs of choking or an inability to keep food down (possible blockage from a swallowed shell)

If your dog ate a big plate of seasoned, garlicky, or fried shrimp — not just a plain piece — call your vet even if they seem okay, especially for a small dog. A quick conversation beats a guessing game every time.
