If you are reading this, you are probably watching an old or ill dog more closely than you ever have — counting the mouthfuls at breakfast, noticing how long it takes them to stand, wondering whether today was a good day or just a less bad one.

That uncertainty is exhausting. A quality of life scale will not make the decision for you, but it can turn a swirl of worry into something you can write down, track and bring to your vet.

## Canine Quality of Life Scale — The Short Answer

**The most widely used canine quality of life scale is the HHHHHMM scale**, developed by veterinary oncologist Dr. Alice Villalobos. It scores seven areas from 0 to 10:

| Category                    | What you are asking                                        |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Hurt**                    | Is pain well controlled? Can your dog breathe comfortably? |
| **Hunger**                  | Are they eating enough, with or without help?              |
| **Hydration**               | Are they drinking enough, or getting dehydrated?           |
| **Hygiene**                 | Can they stay clean after toileting, and free of sores?    |
| **Happiness**               | Do they still show joy and interest in family, toys, food? |
| **Mobility**                | Can they get up and move around, with or without help?     |
| **More good days than bad** | Across the week, which outnumber the other?                |

According to the [original scale published by Dr. Villalobos](https://caringpathways.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/QualityofLifeScale.pdf), **a total over 35 out of 70 represents acceptable quality of life** to continue with hospice or palliative care.

It was built for dogs with serious illness or at the end of life, and it has since been studied more widely — a [2023 study published in _Animals_](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10044252/) validated the HHHHHMM scale with 314 companion animal owners in Italy.

## How to Score Each Category Honestly

The scale is simple. Scoring it honestly is the hard part, because love pushes the numbers up. These prompts help.

### Hurt

The original scale puts pain control and breathing first, and notes that **trouble breathing outweighs all other concerns**. Dogs hide pain well, so look for indirect signs: panting at rest, restlessness at night, reluctance to lie down, a hunched posture, licking one spot, flinching when touched, or snapping when they never used to.

If you are not sure whether heavy breathing is pain, heat or something else, our guide to [calming a panting dog](/blog/how-to-calm-a-panting-dog) covers the difference and when it needs a vet.

### Hunger

Is your dog eating enough to hold their weight? Does warming food, hand feeding or a softer texture help? A dog who takes a few bites and walks away is scoring lower than one who finishes the bowl slowly. Older dogs often do better on softer food — see our guide to [soft dog food for senior dogs](/blog/soft-dog-food-for-senior-dogs).

### Hydration

Watch the water bowl, and check the gums — they should feel moist, not tacky. For dogs not drinking enough, the original scale suggests fluids given under the skin once or twice a day — something many vets can teach you to do at home.

### Hygiene

Can your dog keep themselves clean after toileting? Are they developing pressure sores from lying in one position? Soft bedding, regular turning for a dog who cannot move, and gentle cleaning all raise this score.

### Happiness

This one is easy to overrate. Ask: does your dog still come to greet you, look for their favorite spot, react to their name, a walk, a treat? Or are they withdrawing, hiding, or seeming anxious or afraid? The [AAHA/IAAHPC End-of-Life Care Guidelines](https://www.aaha.org/wp-content/uploads/globalassets/02-guidelines/end-of-life/2016_aaha_iaahpc_eolc_guidelines.pdf) specifically suggest watching for signs of withdrawal, depression or resignation, and keeping a dog involved in daily routines and family life.

### Mobility

Can your dog stand up on their own, walk to the door, go outside to toilet? Do they need a harness or help? The scale itself makes an important point here: **a dog with limited mobility who is still alert, happy and responsive can have a good quality of life**, as long as their caregivers can support them.

### More good days than bad

Step back and look at the whole week. When bad days start to outnumber good ones, the scale notes, quality of life may be too compromised.

## How to Use the Score Well

**Track the trend, not the day.** Every ill dog has rough afternoons. Score at the same time each day or every few days, write it down, and look at the line over one to two weeks.

**Watch the lowest category, not just the total.** A dog can total 40 while scoring a 2 on pain. That is not an acceptable quality of life — it is a pain problem that needs treating today.

**Have two people score separately.** The person who does most of the care often sees things others miss, and sometimes grows used to changes that are obvious to someone who visits less.

**Define your dog's "good day" in advance.** While your dog is still doing reasonably well, write down three or four things that make them _them_ — the walk to the corner, stealing a sock, meeting you at the door. When most of those are gone, that list is a clear-eyed reminder written by the version of you who was less overwhelmed.

**Bring the log to your vet.** A two-week record of scores is far more useful in an appointment than "I think he's been a bit worse."

## What the Score Does Not Do

A quality of life scale is a tool for seeing clearly. It is not a verdict.

- **It does not diagnose.** A sudden drop can have a treatable cause — pain, nausea, an infection, a medication side effect.
- **It does not replace pain management.** The AAHA/IAAHPC guidelines state that pain should be addressed as soon as it is suspected. They also note there is **no evidence that pain suddenly intensifies during active dying**, which many owners find reassuring.
- **It does not make the decision for you.** Those guidelines describe end-of-life decisions as a collaboration between the family and the veterinary team, and they recognise that choosing between hospice care and euthanasia is the owner's right and responsibility.

Ask your vet about palliative or hospice care if your dog has a terminal or progressive condition. Pain relief, anti-nausea medication, appetite support, fluids and home adjustments can raise several scores at once, sometimes for weeks or months.

## What This Means for Your Dog

For a senior dog who is simply slowing down, the scale is a useful check-in every few weeks. It helps you notice when "old" has quietly become "uncomfortable", and it pairs well with the twice-yearly senior checkups vets recommend — something we cover in our guide to [pet wellness plans](/blog/pet-wellness-plans).

For a dog with a serious illness, daily or every-other-day scoring gives you and your vet a shared language. The number matters less than the conversation it starts.

And if you are nearing the end, be kind to yourself. There is rarely a perfect moment, and the fact that you are watching this carefully says a great deal about the life your dog has had. The [AVMA's pet loss resources](https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/pet-owners/petcare/pet-loss) are there for the part that comes after.

## Practical Takeaways

- Score all seven HHHHHMM categories from 0 to 10; over 35 out of 70 is the original threshold for acceptable quality of life.
- Treat any very low single score — especially pain or breathing — as urgent, whatever the total.
- Score regularly, write it down, and watch the trend over one to two weeks.
- Have a second person score independently.
- Use the log to plan with your vet, including palliative options.

Every good year starts with the everyday basics, and so does every good last chapter — comfort, food they will eat, time with their people.
