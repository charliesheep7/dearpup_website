## The Short Answer

No — and this is one of the few food questions where the answer is actually firm.

Fish itself is good for dogs. The [American Kennel Club](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-fish/) calls it a healthy protein source rich in omega-3s, and it shows up in commercial dog food for good reason. **The problem is not the fish. It is the raw.**

Cooking removes three specific risks that raw fish carries, and gives up almost nothing in return. There is no meaningful nutrient in raw fish that a dog loses by having it cooked.

## Risk One: Salmon Poisoning Disease

This is the one most owners have never heard of, and it is the reason vets in some regions are firm about raw fish.

Salmon poisoning disease is caused by *Neorickettsia helminthoeca*, a bacterium carried by a flatworm that encysts in freshwater fish. [VCA Animal Hospitals](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/salmon-poisoning) explains the chain: a dog eats raw or undercooked fish, the infected flukes embed in the intestine, and the bacteria enter the bloodstream.

A few specifics worth knowing:

- **It comes from salmon, trout, steelhead, and Pacific giant salamanders.** Not fish generally.
- **It is regional.** VCA notes it is mostly seen in Washington, Oregon, northern California, and southern Vancouver Island.
- **Signs appear within about a week.**
- **Untreated, most dogs die within two weeks.**
- **Treated, most dogs recover completely.**

That last pair is the important part. This is a serious disease with an excellent outcome *if you catch it*. The dogs who do badly are the ones whose owners did not connect a fish dinner a week ago to a dog who now seems unwell.

Signs include lack of appetite, vomiting, diarrhea that may contain blood, fever, weakness, enlarged lymph nodes, weight loss, and discharge from the eyes or nose. More advanced cases add rapid breathing, elevated heart rate, muscle tremors, and seizures.

If you live in the Pacific Northwest and your dog scavenged a fish carcass off a riverbank, that is a vet call — not a wait-and-see.

## Risk Two: Thiaminase and Vitamin B1

This one is slower and easier to miss because it looks like nothing for a while.

Raw fish contains thiaminase, an enzyme that destroys thiamine — vitamin B1. A [review in *Veterinary Sciences*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5753639/) notes that thiaminase is present in the flesh of dead fish and in some shellfish, and that **the risk of thiamine deficiency exists in both raw and inadequately cooked fish-based diets.** Heat denatures the enzyme, but not all fish respond identically, which is why "lightly seared" is not the same as cooked.

The deficiency unfolds in stages. Early on, within one to two weeks, dogs show vomiting, lethargy, and reduced appetite — which is easy to write off as a bad week. If it continues, the neurological signs arrive: unsteadiness, weakness in the hind end, abnormal eye movement, delayed pupil response, and eventually blindness or an inability to stand. Advanced cases progress to seizures and heart rhythm abnormalities.

This is not a risk from one raw fillet. It is a risk from a raw-fish-heavy diet fed over weeks, which is exactly what some home-prepared and raw feeding plans look like.

## Risk Three: Bacteria

The [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/get-facts-raw-pet-food-diets-can-be-dangerous-you-and-your-pet) tested 196 raw pet food samples and found **15 positive for Salmonella and 32 positive for *Listeria monocytogenes*.** Its position is direct: raw pet food is more likely than processed food to carry harmful bacteria, and the single best way to prevent infection is not to feed a raw diet.

Two things make this different from the other risks.

First, it is a household problem, not just a dog problem. The FDA notes that infected pets can shed bacteria in their feces and saliva **without showing any signs of being sick**, contaminating the environment around them. If there is a toddler, an elderly relative, or anyone immunocompromised in the house, the raw bowl is their problem too.

Second, freezing does not fix it. Freezing is often offered as the workaround for raw feeding, and it does address parasites. It does not kill Salmonella or Listeria. Frozen raw fish is safer than fresh raw fish and still not as safe as cooked.

The same logic applies to other raw proteins — we covered it for eggs in [can dogs eat raw eggs](/blog/can-dogs-eat-raw-eggs).

## Bones Are a Separate Problem Entirely

Worth separating out, because it applies to cooked fish too.

Fish bones are small, brittle, and sharp. The AKC is blunt about the risk: they can lodge in the mouth, throat, stomach, or intestines, and the sharp edges can perforate the intestinal wall. A perforation is emergency surgery.

Debone thoroughly, or use a fish where the bones are genuinely soft — canned sardines packed in water are the usual recommendation for exactly this reason.

## How to Serve Fish the Right Way

The good version of this is easy:

1. **Cook it fully.** Baked, steamed, or grilled. Not seared, not raw, not "sushi grade" — that grading is about parasite risk for humans, not about your dog's thiamine.
2. **Plain.** No oil, butter, salt, garlic, or onion. Garlic and onion are genuinely toxic, unlike the fish itself — see our list of [foods toxic to dogs](/blog/toxic-foods-for-dogs).
3. **Debone completely**, or pick a soft-boned option.
4. **Pick a low-mercury species.** Salmon, whitefish, herring, flounder, and Arctic char are the AKC's picks. Skip tuna as a staple.
5. **Keep it to about 10% of daily calories** unless your vet has built fish into an actual meal plan.

For more on which species work and how much, see [can dogs eat fish](/blog/can-dogs-eat-fish), [can dogs eat tilapia](/blog/can-dogs-eat-tilapia), and [can dogs eat seafood](/blog/can-dogs-eat-seafood).

## If Your Dog Already Ate Raw Fish

Do not panic — most dogs who grab a piece of raw salmon off the counter are completely fine.

Do extend your watch window. The instinct is to monitor for a day. With raw fish, the two conditions that matter show up on a **one-to-two week** timeline, not an overnight one.

Call your vet if you see fever, repeated vomiting, diarrhea with blood, swollen glands under the jaw, unusual lethargy, or any wobbliness or coordination problems. Mention the raw fish explicitly, and mention where it came from — a Pacific Northwest river changes what your vet is looking for.

Raw fish is one of the rare cases where the cautious answer and the correct answer are the same. Cook it, bone it, and your dog gets all of the upside and none of this.
