Obedience training sounds formal, but at heart it is just teaching your dog a shared language. A dog that reliably sits, waits, and comes when called is safer, calmer, and a lot easier to take everywhere.

The good news: you do not need special talent or expensive gear. You need a few treats, a few minutes a day, and a plan. Here is that plan, step by step.

## What You'll Need

- **Small, soft treats.** Pea-sized pieces your dog loves. Soft treats are eaten quickly, so the session keeps moving.
- **A standard 6-foot leash and a flat collar or harness.**
- **A quiet room to start.** Add distractions later, not on day one.
- **A marker.** A clicker or a short word like "yes" that tells your dog the exact moment they got it right. The [AKC's guide to clicker training](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/training/clicker-training-your-dog-mark-and-reward/) explains how to "charge" a marker before you use it.
- **Two to three short sessions a day.** A few minutes each beats one long, frustrating session.

Before you begin, pick your method. **Use reward-based training.** The [American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior's position statement](https://avsab.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/AVSAB-Humane-Dog-Training-Position-Statement-2021.pdf) recommends reward-based methods only, and warns that aversive tools like leash corrections and shock collars can increase fear and aggression. The research backs this up. A [2020 study in _PLOS ONE_](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7743949/) that followed 92 companion dogs found that those trained with aversive methods showed more stress behaviors during training and had higher cortisol levels afterward than dogs trained with rewards.

## Step 1: Teach a Name and Attention Cue

Every other command depends on your dog looking at you first.

1. Say your dog's name once in a happy voice.
2. The moment they look at you, mark ("yes") and give a treat.
3. Repeat 5 to 10 times, then end the session.

Never use their name to scold them. You want the name to mean "good things happen when I look at my person."

## Step 2: Sit

Sit is the easiest place to start and a building block for polite manners.

1. Hold a treat at your dog's nose.
2. Slowly move it up and slightly back over their head. As the nose goes up, the rear goes down.
3. The instant their bottom touches the floor, mark and reward.
4. After a few reps, add the word "sit" just before you lure.
5. Gradually fade the lure so a hand signal or the word alone works.

## Step 3: Down

1. Ask for a sit.
2. Lower a treat from your dog's nose straight down to the floor between their paws, then slowly out along the ground.
3. When their elbows touch the floor, mark and reward.

If your dog pops up, you moved the treat too fast. Slow down and reward smaller steps, like lowering their head.

## Step 4: Stay

Stay teaches self-control. Build it using the three Ds: **duration, distance, and distraction**, and only change one at a time.

1. Ask for a sit or down.
2. Show a flat palm, say "stay," wait one second, then mark and reward while your dog is still in position.
3. Slowly stretch the wait to 5, 10, then 30 seconds.
4. Next, take one step back and return to reward. Add distance slowly.
5. Finally, add small distractions like dropping a toy.

Always go back to your dog to reward, and use a release word like "okay" or "free" so they know when the stay is over.

## Step 5: Come (Recall)

A reliable recall can keep your dog out of traffic, so it is worth extra effort.

1. Start indoors, a few feet away. Say "come" in an upbeat voice and back away a step or two.
2. When your dog reaches you, reward generously. Recall deserves your best treats.
3. Practice on a long line outside before trusting your dog off leash.

**Never call your dog to you for something they dislike**, like a bath or the end of a park visit. If "come" predicts bad news, your dog will learn to ignore it.

## Step 6: Loose-Leash Walking

Pulling is one of the most common complaints owners have.

1. Walk with treats at your side.
2. Reward often when the leash is loose and your dog is near your leg.
3. If your dog pulls, stop and stand still. Move forward again only when the leash goes slack.
4. Change direction often to keep your dog paying attention.

Expect this one to take weeks. Walks are exciting, and your dog is learning to ignore a world full of smells.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Repeating the cue.** Saying "sit, sit, SIT" teaches your dog the cue is "sit sit sit." Say it once, then help them.
- **Sessions that run too long.** Stop while your dog is still keen. Ending on a win keeps them eager for next time.
- **Moving too fast.** If your dog fails twice in a row, make it easier.
- **Only training in the kitchen.** Dogs do not generalize well. Practice in the yard, on walks, and at the park.
- **Punishing mistakes.** Veterinary behaviorists see no role for aversive training, as [JAVMA News reported](https://www.avma.org/javma-news/2021-11-01/veterinary-behaviorists-no-role-aversive-dog-training-practices). Redirect and reward the behavior you want instead.

## Tips for Different Ages and Dogs

### Puppies

Puppies have tiny attention spans, so aim for sessions of just a few minutes. Pair obedience with socialization, which [VCA notes](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/puppy-behavior-and-training-socialization-and-fear-prevention) is most important in the first few months of life. Our [puppy training tips](/blog/puppy-training-tips) cover potty training and crate training too.

### Adult and rescue dogs

Older dogs learn well, but they may need time to trust you. Start in a quiet space and keep rewards high. If your dog seems anxious, our guide to [how to calm a dog down](/blog/how-to-calm-a-dog-down-instantly) can help.

### Food-driven vs toy-driven dogs

Some dogs will do anything for a bit of chicken. Others work harder for a quick game of tug. Use whatever your dog values most.

### Watch the treat calories

Training treats add up fast. Use tiny pieces, or set aside part of your dog's daily kibble for training. Our [dog calorie calculator](/blog/dog-calorie-calculator) can help you stay on track.

## Taking It Further

Once your dog has the basics, a class can polish them. The [AKC Canine Good Citizen program](https://www.akc.org/products-services/training-programs/canine-good-citizen/) tests everyday manners like walking politely through a crowd and staying calm around other dogs, and it makes a great goal to work toward. For a broader overview of training from start to finish, read our guide on [how to train your dog](/blog/how-to-train-your-dog).

## Practical Takeaways

- Use reward-based training; it is the method veterinary behaviorists recommend
- Start with attention, sit, and down, then build stay, come, and leash walking
- Keep sessions short and frequent, and end on a success
- Change one thing at a time: duration, distance, or distraction
- Practice in many places so your dog learns the cue works everywhere
