Protein is the number everyone reads first on a dog food bag and the number almost nobody reads correctly. A 26% on one bag and a 10% on another can mean the same thing. A "senior" formula can legally contain less protein than the food your dog ate at three years old. And the widely repeated warning that high protein wrecks kidneys turns out to be about a specific group of already-sick dogs.

Here is what the number actually means, how much your dog needs, and where the real decisions are.

## The Short Answer

For a healthy dog, the official floors are low and the useful range sits above them.

[AAFCO's nutrient profiles](https://www.aafco.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Pet_Food_Report_2013_Annual-Appendix_B.pdf) set the minimum crude protein at **18% on a dry matter basis for adult maintenance and 22.5% for growth and reproduction**. Any food sold as complete and balanced has to clear the bar for its stated life stage.

Read that as a safety floor, not a recommendation. It is the level set to prevent visible deficiency in a healthy dog eating that food and nothing else. It says very little about what will keep a ten-year-old Labrador carrying muscle, or fuel a dog that runs six miles a day.

## Why the Bag's Number Can Mislead You

### Dry Matter Is the Only Fair Comparison

The guaranteed analysis on a bag or can reports protein **as fed**, water included. Since dry food is around 10% moisture and wet food is around 75%, the two numbers live on completely different scales.

To compare them, convert to dry matter:

1. Find the moisture percentage on the label.
2. Subtract it from 100 to get dry matter.
3. Divide crude protein by dry matter, then multiply by 100.

A canned food listing 10% protein and 75% moisture works out to 10 ÷ 25 × 100 = **40% protein on a dry matter basis** — far higher than the 26% kibble it is sitting next to on the shelf. Skip this step and you will consistently underrate wet food.

### "Crude Protein" Measures Nitrogen, Not Quality

Crude protein is calculated from nitrogen content. That makes it a good measure of quantity and a poor measure of usefulness, because it cannot distinguish highly digestible chicken from a poorly digestible by-product filler with the same nitrogen.

What dogs actually need is a supply of specific amino acids. The [Merck Veterinary Manual](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/management-and-nutrition/nutrition-small-animals/nutritional-requirements-of-small-animals) frames protein requirements in exactly those terms — essential amino acids that the dog cannot make itself and has to eat.

This is why two foods showing the same percentage are not interchangeable, and why the ingredient list matters as much as the analysis. Our guide to [dog food ingredients](/blog/dog-food-ingredients) covers how to read the rest of the panel.

## What This Means at Each Life Stage

### Puppies

Growth is the most demanding phase, which is why the minimum jumps to 22.5%. Puppies build tissue as well as maintain it, and a shortfall during growth is not something you can make up for later.

Large-breed puppies are the exception that needs care — not on protein, but on calcium and overall energy density, which is why [large-breed puppy food](/blog/puppy-food-for-large-breed-puppies) exists as its own category.

### Adults

Most healthy adult dogs are comfortably served by mainstream foods, which usually sit well above the 18% floor. The decision points are activity and body condition rather than the number itself.

A dog that is gaining fat while losing muscle tone is often eating enough calories and not enough protein. A dog holding good muscle on its current food does not need a higher-protein bag because a marketing page said so.

### Senior Dogs

This is where the biggest misunderstanding lives. **There is no AAFCO nutrient profile for senior dogs at all** — the category is a marketing label, and a "senior" food only has to meet the 18% adult maintenance minimum.

Meanwhile, older dogs tend to lose muscle mass with age and become less efficient at using dietary protein. Reducing their protein because the bag says "senior" can work directly against them. Unless your vet has diagnosed a condition that calls for restriction, adequate or generous protein is usually the better default for an older dog — our guide to [senior dog food](/blog/senior-dog-food) goes into the wider picture.

## The Kidney Question, Answered Plainly

The belief that high protein damages kidneys is one of the stickiest myths in dog nutrition, and it has a real origin: controlled protein is part of how vets manage dogs **already diagnosed** with chronic kidney disease.

That is a treatment protocol for sick kidneys, not a preventive rule for healthy ones. There is no good evidence that feeding a healthy dog with normal kidney function a higher-protein diet causes kidney disease.

Two practical implications:

- If your dog is healthy, do not restrict protein out of caution. You may cost them muscle for no benefit.
- If your dog has been diagnosed with kidney disease, follow the therapeutic diet your vet prescribes precisely. This is one of the places where a prescription diet genuinely outperforms an over-the-counter choice.

Dogs with liver disease, pancreatitis, or certain urinary stones also have specific protein considerations. Those are vet conversations, not label conversations.

## Practical Takeaways

- **Convert to dry matter before comparing anything.** Otherwise wet and dry food numbers are not on the same scale.
- Treat **18% adult / 22.5% growth** as floors, not goals.
- "Senior" is a marketing term with no nutrient profile behind it. Check the actual number.
- Crude protein tells you quantity. The ingredient list tells you quality. You need both.
- Judge by your dog, not the bag: muscle tone over the spine and hips, energy, and coat condition are the real readouts. Our guide on [how much to feed your dog](/blog/how-much-food-should-i-feed-my-dog) helps with the portion side.
- Do not restrict protein in a healthy dog on the strength of the kidney myth. Do follow your vet exactly if kidney disease is diagnosed.

Protein is worth getting right, but it is one number among several. A food that gets protein perfect and fat, fibre, and digestibility wrong is not a good food — and your dog's muscle tone six months from now will tell you more than any percentage on the front of the bag.
