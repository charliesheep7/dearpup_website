## The Short Answer

"All life stages" is not a marketing phrase. It is a regulated claim with a specific meaning, and the meaning surprises most owners:

**An all life stages food is built to puppy standards, not adult standards.**

To carry the label, a food must meet the AAFCO nutrient profile for *growth and reproduction* — the profile designed for puppies and for pregnant or nursing dogs. Since that profile sits above the adult maintenance minimums, a food that clears it can legally be fed to a dog of any age.

That is the whole trick. It is not a food formulated for every life stage. It is a food formulated for the most demanding one.

## What the Label Is Actually Telling You

Every complete dog food carries an AAFCO nutritional adequacy statement, usually in small print on the back. There are three life-stage categories:

- **Adult maintenance** — for grown dogs who are not pregnant or nursing
- **Growth and reproduction** — for puppies and for pregnant or lactating females
- **All life stages** — meets the growth and reproduction profile, so covers everything

The gap between them is real. According to [PetMD's breakdown of AAFCO standards](https://www.petmd.com/dog/nutrition/What-Is-AAFCO-and-What-Does-It-Do), the dry-matter minimums are:

| | Adult maintenance | Growth & reproduction |
|---|---|---|
| Crude protein | 18% | 22.5% |
| Crude fat | 5.5% | 8.5% |

**The fat minimum is over 50% higher.** Fat is the most calorie-dense macronutrient, which is why an all life stages food usually packs more calories into the same cup than an adult formula does.

There is a second thing the statement tells you: *how* the claim was substantiated. Either the food was formulated on paper to hit the profile, or it passed an actual AAFCO feeding trial. The feeding trial language reads "animal feeding tests using AAFCO procedures substantiate..." and is the stronger of the two.

## Who It Genuinely Suits

All life stages food is a good fit when:

- **You have a multi-dog household with mixed ages** and want one bag. This is the real convenience, and it is legitimate.
- **Your dog is active** — working dogs, sport dogs, dogs with long daily mileage.
- **Your dog struggles to keep weight on.** The higher calorie density is a feature here.
- **You are feeding a small or medium breed puppy.** The growth profile is exactly what they need.

## Who Should Probably Skip It

Three groups where the richer profile works against you.

**Sedentary or weight-prone adults.** A neutered adult dog with a couple of short walks a day does not need growth-level fat. You can compensate by feeding less, but then you are serving a smaller bowl of a richer food — which tends to leave dogs hungry. An adult maintenance formula lets you serve more volume for the same calories.

**Most seniors.** Older dogs are usually less active and benefit from lower calorie density while holding protein up. All life stages does the opposite of the first half of that. See [senior dog food](/blog/senior-dog-food) for what to look for instead.

**Large breed puppies — unless the label says so explicitly.** This is the one that actually matters.

## The Large Breed Puppy Exception

If you take one thing from this article, take this.

A standard all life stages food is **not** automatically appropriate for a puppy who will grow past 70 pounds. The reason is calcium.

Large breed puppies that grow too quickly face elevated risk of developmental orthopedic disease — hip dysplasia, elbow dysplasia, and osteochondritis dissecans. [PetMD](https://www.petmd.com/dog/nutrition/best-puppy-food-large-breeds) explains that AAFCO revised its guidelines in 2018 so a large-breed puppy food must carry a **lower maximum calcium level** than foods for all other life stages, including regular puppy food. Capping calcium slows the growth rate, and a slower growth rate means fewer joint problems later.

A generic all life stages food is held to the wider calcium range. It can be perfectly compliant and still be wrong for a Great Dane puppy.

So check for the specific clause. The statement you want includes growth of large size dogs, 70 pounds or more as an adult. If it is absent, the food is not validated for your large breed puppy — regardless of how good the rest of the label looks.

[VCA](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/nutritional-requirements-of-large-and-giant-breed-puppies) also notes these puppies may stay on puppy food for up to two years, so this is not a short window. More detail in [puppy food for large breed puppies](/blog/puppy-food-for-large-breed-puppies) and [large breed dog food](/blog/large-breed-dog-food).

## How to Decide in the Store

Six checks, in order:

1. **Find the AAFCO statement.** Back of the bag, small print. If there is not one, put the bag down.
2. **Read which life stage it names.** All life stages, growth and reproduction, or adult maintenance.
3. **If you have a large breed puppy,** look for the 70-pound growth clause. No clause, no purchase.
4. **Check kcal per cup.** All life stages foods are usually denser, so your old portion will overfeed.
5. **Match it to activity, not age alone.** An eight-year-old agility dog and an eight-year-old couch dog need different foods.
6. **Recalculate portions when you switch.** Same cup, different calories — this is how dogs quietly gain a pound a month.

## The Bottom Line

All life stages food is honest about what it is, as long as you know how to read it: a food built to the puppy standard and cleared for everyone else by consequence.

For an active dog or a mixed-age household, that is genuinely convenient. For a large breed puppy without the right clause, or a senior who needs fewer calories, the convenience costs something. The AAFCO statement is four lines of small print, and it settles the question faster than anything on the front of the bag.
