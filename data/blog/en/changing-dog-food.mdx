Maybe your puppy is growing up, your vet mentioned weight, or the bag you have bought for years just got reformulated. Whatever the reason, changing dog food is one of those jobs that looks simple and can go sideways fast.

The good news: most tummy trouble after a food switch comes from doing it too quickly, and that part is completely in your control.

## Changing Dog Food — The Short Answer

**Mix the new food into the old food gradually over about 7 to 10 days.** Start with a small amount of new food, increase it every couple of days, and watch your dog's stool and appetite as you go.

The [AKC's guide to switching dog foods](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/right-way-switch-dog-foods/) lays out a 5 to 7 day schedule that moves from 25% new food to 100%. [PetMD](https://www.petmd.com/dog/nutrition/how-to-switch-your-dogs-food) suggests a similar week-long plan starting at just 10% new food, and both note that sensitive dogs may need longer.

A sudden swap can bring on vomiting, diarrhea, gas or a dog who simply refuses to eat. A slow switch gives the gut time to adjust.

## Good Reasons to Change Your Dog's Food

Changing food is not something you need to do on a schedule. But there are solid reasons to make the move:

- **Life stage.** Puppies need a growth or all-life-stages formula, and many owners move to an adult food once growth slows. Older dogs may benefit from a food chosen for their changing needs — our [senior dog food guide](/blog/senior-dog-food) covers what to look for.
- **Suspected food allergies.** Itchy skin, paw licking and recurring ear infections can point to a food allergy. That calls for a vet-guided elimination diet rather than random brand-hopping; see [dog food for food allergies](/blog/dog-food-for-food-allergies).
- **Weight.** A dog who is gaining or losing weight may need a different calorie density. If your dog is getting thinner without trying, read [dog losing weight](/blog/dog-losing-weight) and talk to your vet before blaming the food.
- **Digestive issues.** Ongoing soft stool or gas sometimes improves on a simpler, more digestible recipe.
- **A vet prescription.** Kidney, urinary and gastrointestinal diets are common reasons for a planned switch.
- **Practical changes.** A recall, a discontinued recipe, or a budget change.

Whatever your reason, check the new bag for a **complete and balanced** statement for your dog's life stage. The [WSAVA's guide to selecting a pet food](https://wsava.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Selecting-a-pet-food-for-your-pet-updated-2021_WSAVA-Global-Nutrition-Toolkit.pdf) is a helpful checklist for questions to ask about any brand.

## The 10-Day Food Transition Schedule

This schedule sits comfortably between the AKC and PetMD plans and gives most dogs a little extra breathing room. Use it for each meal, not just once a day.

| Day   | Old Food | New Food |
| ----- | -------- | -------- |
| 1–2   | 75%      | 25%      |
| 3–4   | 60%      | 40%      |
| 5–6   | 50%      | 50%      |
| 7–8   | 25%      | 75%      |
| 9     | 10%      | 90%      |
| 10    | 0%       | 100%     |

If your dog is a cast-iron-stomach type, you can compress this toward the AKC's 7-day version. If they are touchy, stretch each step to three or four days.

### How to Make the Switch Go Smoothly

1. **Buy the new food before the old bag runs out.** You need both on hand for the whole transition.
2. **Mix thoroughly.** Stir the foods together so your dog cannot pick out their favorite pieces.
3. **Keep total portions the same size in calories, not scoops.** Foods differ in calorie density, so check the new feeding guide. Our [dog calorie calculator](/blog/dog-calorie-calculator) can help you set a daily target.
4. **Hold the treats steady.** Adding new chews or table scraps during a food switch makes it hard to tell what caused any upset.
5. **Check the stool every day.** Firm, well-formed stool means you can move to the next step. Softer stool means stay put.

**If stool softens, step back to the last ratio that worked** and hold there for a few days before moving forward again. Both the AKC and PetMD suggest slowing down when your dog shows signs of upset, so stretching the switch toward two weeks is perfectly fine.

## When a Slower Switch Makes Sense

Some dogs do better with a 10 to 14 day transition, or even longer:

- **Dogs with sensitive stomachs.** If your dog already has a history of soft stools or vomiting, go slow and consider splitting the day's food into smaller meals. Our guide to [wet dog food for sensitive stomachs](/blog/wet-dog-food-for-sensitive-stomach) has more on choosing a gentle recipe.
- **Senior dogs.** Older dogs are more likely to have an underlying health condition that a sudden change could stir up, so a slower pace and closer watching is sensible.
- **Puppies.** Their growing bodies depend on steady nutrition, and PetMD specifically advises against quick switches for puppies.
- **Dogs with health conditions.** Dogs with kidney disease, pancreatitis, diabetes or allergies should follow a transition plan from their vet.
- **Big changes in fat or format.** Moving from a lean kibble to a rich canned or fresh diet is a bigger jump for the gut than switching between two similar kibbles.

## Switching Between Wet, Dry and Fresh Food

Changing the format of your dog's food follows the same gradual principle, with a few extra things to think about.

### Dry to wet

Wet food holds much more water and fewer calories per bite, so a scoop-for-scoop swap can underfeed your dog. Use the calorie counts on both labels. Our [wet vs dry dog food guide](/blog/wet-dog-food) explains the trade-offs.

### Wet to dry

Some dogs who love canned food are unenthusiastic about kibble. PetMD suggests that if your dog likes wet food, you choose a new wet food rather than trying to move to dry. If you do need to move to dry, mixing a little of the old wet food on top during the switch can help.

### Kibble to fresh

Fresh and gently cooked diets are often richer and more palatable, so dogs usually eat them eagerly, which is exactly why you should go slowly. Read our [fresh dog food guide](/blog/fresh-dog-food) before you start, and portion by calories rather than by eye.

Fresh food also needs refrigeration, so only mix what your dog will eat at that meal.

## Signs You Are Changing Food Too Fast

Mild, short-lived changes are common during a switch. Watch for:

- **Soft stool or diarrhea** — the most common sign
- **Gas** that is more frequent or smellier than usual
- **Vomiting**, especially shortly after meals
- **Eating less or refusing the new food**
- **Picking around the new food** in the bowl

For mild soft stool or gas, slow down: go back one step, hold for a few days, and continue more gradually.

## When to Call Your Vet

A food change should not make your dog genuinely unwell. PetMD advises calling your vet if your dog still refuses the new food after two days, or if vomiting or diarrhea develops during the switch.

**Call your vet promptly** if you notice:

- Vomiting more than once, or diarrhea lasting more than a day or two
- Blood in the stool or vomit, or black, tarry stool
- Lethargy, weakness or a painful, bloated belly
- Refusing all food for more than a day or two
- Any of these signs in a puppy, a senior dog, or a dog with a known health condition

If the problems keep coming back even with a slow switch, the new food may simply not suit your dog — or something else may be going on. Our guide on [how to tell if your dog is sick](/blog/how-can-you-tell-if-your-dog-is-sick) covers other signs worth watching.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Switching the moment a new bag arrives.** Plan the transition before you open it.
- **Changing food because of one bad day.** An occasional soft stool is normal; a pattern is what matters.
- **Rotating flavors constantly.** For a sensitive dog, every new flavor is effectively a new food.
- **Giving up too early.** A good food still needs a few weeks after the switch to show what it can do for skin, coat and stool.
- **Guessing portions.** Different foods, different calories. Check the label.

## Practical Takeaways

- Switch over **7 to 10 days** for most dogs, and 10 to 14 days or longer for sensitive dogs, seniors and puppies.
- Keep portions matched by **calories**, not scoops.
- Let your dog's stool decide the pace. Soft stool means step back.
- Choose a **complete and balanced** food for your dog's life stage.
- Call your vet for vomiting, diarrhea that will not settle, blood, lethargy or refusing food.

A patient switch takes a little planning, but it saves a lot of carpet cleaning — and it gives the new food a fair chance to work.
