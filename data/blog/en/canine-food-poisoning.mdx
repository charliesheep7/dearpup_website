Your dog got into the kitchen bin overnight, and now they are pacing, drooling and bringing up something unrecognisable on the rug. Is this a bad afternoon, or a vet emergency?

Usually it is the first. Occasionally it is the second, and the difference comes down to a handful of signs worth knowing before you need them.

## Canine Food Poisoning — The Short Answer

**Yes, dogs get food poisoning**, even with stomachs that cope with things ours never could. Vets often call the everyday version "garbage gut" or dietary indiscretion: a dog eats something spoiled, and the gut reacts with vomiting and diarrhea.

Most mild cases pass within a day or two with rest and a bland diet. But three situations need a vet quickly: **tremors or seizures**, signs your dog is getting dehydrated, and symptoms that are not improving after a day or two.

Food poisoning is different from eating something _toxic_ — chocolate, grapes, xylitol, onions. Those cause harm even when perfectly fresh, and they are covered in our list of [toxic foods for dogs](/blog/toxic-foods-for-dogs). This article is about food that turned harmful because of bacteria or mold.

## What Usually Causes It

### Spoiled food and bin raids

The most common story. Leftovers past their best, meat scraps from the trash, a dead animal on a walk, another animal's vomit. As food breaks down, bacteria multiply and some produce toxins that irritate the gut.

### Bacteria in raw meat and raw pet food

Raw meat commonly carries bacteria such as Salmonella, Listeria and E. coli. When the [FDA's Center for Veterinary Medicine](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/get-facts-raw-pet-food-diets-can-be-dangerous-you-and-your-pet) tested **196 commercial raw dog and cat food samples, 15 were positive for Salmonella and 32 for Listeria monocytogenes**.

According to [VCA Animal Hospitals](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/dogs-and-raw-food-diets), salmonellosis in dogs can cause vomiting, diarrhea that is often bloody, fever, loss of appetite and lethargy. VCA also points out that dogs can carry both Salmonella and Listeria **without showing any symptoms** — which matters for the people they live with, too.

### Moldy food

This is the one to take most seriously. Mold growing on bread, cheese, nuts, leftovers and compost can produce tremorgenic mycotoxins — fungal toxins that affect the nervous system.

[Pet Poison Helpline](https://www.petpoisonhelpline.com/poison/mycotoxin/) notes that signs can appear within minutes to hours and include vomiting, agitation, incoordination, tremors and seizures. A dog who got into the compost heap and starts trembling needs a vet now, not a bland diet.

## Signs of Food Poisoning to Watch For

**Common, usually mild:**

- Vomiting, especially soon after eating
- Diarrhea
- Drooling or lip-licking
- Gurgling stomach and gas
- Skipping a meal
- Seeming quieter or more tired than usual

**Signs that need a vet the same day:**

- Tremors, twitching, wobbliness or seizures
- Vomiting or diarrhea that goes on past about 24 hours
- Blood in vomit or stool, or black tarry stool
- Pale gums, a swollen or painful belly, or collapse
- Repeated retching with nothing coming up
- Signs of dehydration: dry sticky gums, sunken eyes, very little urine
- Fever or marked lethargy
- Any of the above in a puppy, a senior dog or a dog with an existing health condition

Dehydration is the main risk in an otherwise mild case. [VCA's guide to gastroenteritis](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/gastroenteritis-in-dogs) notes that it **can develop quickly once vomiting and diarrhea persist for more than 24 hours** — faster in small dogs and puppies, who have less fluid in reserve.

## What to Do If You Think Your Dog Has Food Poisoning

1. **Remove the source.** Close the bin, clear the counter, check the garden and compost. Try to work out what was eaten and roughly how much, and when.
2. **Check for red flags.** If you see tremors, seizures, collapse, blood, or a painful swollen belly, go straight to a vet.
3. **Call for advice if you are unsure.** Your vet is the first call. If you suspect something toxic rather than just spoiled, [ASPCA Animal Poison Control](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control) is available 24 hours a day at (888) 426-4435; a consultation fee may apply.
4. **Do not make your dog vomit** unless a vet or poison control tells you to. Some substances cause more damage coming back up, and home methods can be dangerous.
5. **Skip human medicines.** Anti-diarrhea and anti-nausea drugs made for people can be harmful to dogs or mask a more serious problem.

### Home care for a mild case

If your dog is an otherwise healthy adult, alert, and keeping water down, many vets are comfortable with careful home care:

- **Offer small amounts of water often** rather than letting them gulp a full bowl, which can trigger more vomiting.
- **Give the stomach a short rest.** VCA notes that food is often withheld for 12 to 24 hours during the early stage of treatment for gastroenteritis — check with your vet before fasting a puppy, a toy breed or a diabetic dog.
- **Reintroduce food gently.** Small portions of something bland, such as plain boiled chicken with plain white rice, several times a day.
- **Return to normal food over a few days**, mixing in more of their usual diet as stools firm up.

If things are not clearly getting better within 48 hours, or anything on the red-flag list appears, take your dog in. VCA advises calling your vet if vomiting and diarrhea do not improve significantly within 48 hours of treatment.

## How to Prevent Food Poisoning

**Dog-proof the bin.** A lidded or locking kitchen bin solves most cases outright. Store it inside a cupboard if your dog has figured out the lid.

**Fence off the compost.** Compost is essentially a mold farm, which makes it one of the riskiest places a dog can dig.

**Handle raw food like raw meat for people.** The FDA recommends washing your hands with soap and water for at least 20 seconds after handling raw pet food, cleaning bowls and surfaces with hot soapy water followed by a disinfectant, and not leaving uneaten raw food sitting out.

**Treat leftovers with the same rules you would use for yourself.** If you would not eat it, your dog should not either. Moldy bread and forgotten cheese belong in a secure bin, not the dog bowl. If you do share food, stick to options on our [safe human food list](/blog/what-human-food-can-dogs-eat).

**Store dog food properly.** Keep kibble in its original bag inside a sealed container, somewhere cool and dry, and throw out anything that smells off or shows mold. Refrigerate opened [wet dog food](/blog/wet-dog-food) and use it within the time on the label.

**Keep an eye on recalls.** The FDA publishes [pet food recalls and withdrawals](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/safety-health/recalls-withdrawals), which is worth a quick check if several dogs you know on the same food fall ill at once.

**Walk with a "leave it".** A solid leave-it command stops a lot of roadside scavenging before it starts — our guide on [how to train your dog](/blog/how-to-train-your-dog) walks through teaching it.

## When to Call Your Vet

Call right away for tremors, seizures, collapse, bloody vomit or stool, a hard or painful belly, or signs of dehydration. Call the same day for any symptoms in a puppy or senior dog, or if your dog may have eaten something moldy, a lot of fatty food, bones, or something with packaging still attached.

For a healthy adult with a single bout of vomiting who is otherwise bright and drinking, it is reasonable to watch closely for a few hours. If you are ever unsure, call. A two-minute conversation with your vet is always cheaper than guessing wrong.
