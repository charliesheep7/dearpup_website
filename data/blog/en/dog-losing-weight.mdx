## The Short Answer

If your dog is losing weight and you did not put them on a diet, that is worth a vet visit rather than a wait-and-see.

The useful number: [PetMD advises booking an appointment](https://www.petmd.com/dog/symptoms/dog-losing-weight) if a dog has **lost more than 10% of their normal body weight, or is dropping 2% or more per week.** For a 30 lb dog, 10% is three pounds — small enough to miss on the sofa, large enough to matter.

Most causes turn out to be treatable, and several are boring. But unintentional weight loss is a symptom rather than a condition, and the value of going early is that the cheap tests are the ones that find most of it.

## First, Confirm It Is Real

Scales lie less than eyes do, but most people do not weigh their dog weekly. Two checks close the gap.

**The rib test.** Put your thumbs on the backbone and spread your fingers across the rib cage. At a healthy weight you feel a thin layer of fat with the ribs right underneath. If the ribs, pelvis, and vertebrae feel prominent with nothing over them, your dog is too thin.

**The knuckle trick**, which the [AKC uses as a home shorthand](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/is-my-dog-underweight/): make a fist and feel your knuckles — that sharpness is underweight. Feel the palm side of the same hand, all padding, and that is overweight. Now lay your hand flat and feel the knuckles. That in-between texture is what ideal feels like.

This is body condition scoring, and vets use it precisely because it works across every breed. [VCA explains the scales](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/body-condition-scores) as a measure of fat relative to muscle and bone. On the 9-point scale, **4 to 5 is ideal**; on the 5-point scale, roughly 2.5 to 3.

A greyhound and a bulldog at the same score look nothing alike. That is the point — pounds are breed-specific, condition is not.

## The Common Causes

Weight loss happens for one of three reasons: not enough going in, not enough being absorbed, or too much being burned.

**Not enough going in.** Dental pain is the quiet leader here, and it hides well — a dog with a painful tooth will still approach the bowl and then eat slowly, drop kibble, or chew on one side. Nausea, a disliked new food, and stress from a household change all land in this bucket too.

**Not enough being absorbed.** Intestinal parasites, inflammatory bowel disease, and other gastrointestinal conditions mean the food goes in and the calories do not stay. Stool changes usually travel with these, though not always.

**Too much being burned.** Metabolic and hormonal disorders raise the burn rate or waste calories outright. Diabetes is the classic version, and the tell is a dog who is hungry and drinking more while getting thinner.

Cancer sits across all three and is the reason vets take unexplained loss seriously in older dogs. [VCA's overview of abnormal weight loss](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/weight-loss-abnormal-in-dogs) walks through how broad the list genuinely is, which is exactly why guessing at home does not work.

And sometimes it really is simple: a food switch to something less calorie-dense, a new walking routine, or summer heat suppressing appetite. ## Red Flags Worth a Same-Day Call

Weight loss on its own means book an appointment. Weight loss alongside any of these means move faster:

- Vomiting or diarrhea, particularly if it is repeating
- Lethargy, or a dog who has stopped doing something they normally love
- Noticeably increased thirst or urination
- Difficulty chewing, dropping food, or bad breath that has changed
- Changes in breathing

**Go to an emergency vet rather than waiting** for severe gastrointestinal signs, collapse, real difficulty moving or breathing, or jaundice — a yellow tinge to the gums, eyes, or the skin inside the ears.

## What the Vet Will Actually Do

Knowing the shape of the visit takes some of the dread out of it.

After a full physical exam, the standard first round is a complete blood count, a serum biochemistry profile, a urinalysis, and parasite testing. [VCA outlines this workup](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/testing-for-weight-loss-in-dogs) as the broad screen that catches the majority of common causes.

That panel either finds the answer or narrows it enough to say what comes next. Bring three things with you: a weight history if you have one, a photo of your dog from a month or two ago, and an honest account of what and how much they are eating.

## Senior Dogs Are a Different Conversation

Older dogs do lose muscle mass gradually, and some flattening of the topline with age is expected.

The trap is treating any weight change as just getting older. Senior dogs carry the highest odds of dental disease, kidney disease, and cancer — the exact conditions that show up as weight loss first. **The threshold for investigating should drop with age, not rise.**

If chewing is the issue, a texture change often helps in the meantime; [softer food for senior dogs](/blog/soft-dog-food-for-senior-dogs) covers the practical options. That is a bridge while you find the cause, not a substitute for finding it.

## Practical Takeaways

- Weigh your dog monthly. A number beats an impression, and vet scales are free to use between visits.
- Run the rib check every few weeks so you know your dog's normal by feel.
- Treat **10% total or 2% per week** as the line where a call is overdue.
- Look in the mouth. Dental pain causes a surprising share of quiet weight loss.
- Note what changed — food, routine, household, season — before the appointment.
- Do not fix it by simply adding food. Feeding more masks the signal without addressing what is underneath it.

Most dogs who lose weight get a diagnosis and get it back. The thing that separates a straightforward outcome from a complicated one is usually how long the weight loss ran before anyone measured it.
