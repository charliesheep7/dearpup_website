## Are Cashews Bad for Dogs? The Short Answer

**Cashews are not toxic to dogs — but they are fatty enough that they make a poor everyday treat.** That distinction matters, because "bad for dogs" usually makes owners think poison, and this is not that.

[PetMD](https://www.petmd.com/dog/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-cashews) states it directly: "Cashews are not toxic to dogs, but there are some things to consider before you give them to your dog as a treat."

So if your dog just ate a cashew off the floor, you can relax. The real question is not whether one cashew is dangerous — it is whether cashews should be a habit. The answer to that one is no.

## Fat Is the Actual Problem

Cashews are rich. That is the whole issue in a sentence.

PetMD explains that **"Cashews contain a high amount of fat, and eating fatty foods repeatedly over time can cause obesity or pancreatitis in dogs."** Pancreatitis is a painful inflammation of the pancreas, and it is not a minor thing to recover from.

The [American Kennel Club](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-nuts/) lands in the same place: **"Cashews are also reportedly safe in small quantities, but both cashews and peanuts are too high in fat to make regular treats."**

Note what both sources emphasize — _repeatedly_ and _regular_. The risk builds with frequency, not with a single nut. A dog who gets a cashew now and then is in a completely different situation from one who gets a small handful every evening.

## Salt, Seasoning, and What to Avoid

Most cashews people actually have in the house are roasted and salted, which is the version to skip. The AKC warns that **"Feeding your dog salted nuts can also lead to water retention and other complications."**

Flavored and seasoned nuts are worse again — honey-roasted, spiced, or chili varieties add sugar and seasonings your dog does not need, and some spice blends contain onion or garlic powder.

If you are going to share, PetMD's guidance is that cashews should be "completely plain." Unsalted, unseasoned, nothing on them.

## The Nut That Actually Is Dangerous

Worth knowing while we are here, because owners often lump all nuts together: the AKC notes that **"certain types of nuts, such as macadamia nuts, are very toxic to dogs."**

That is a real distinction. Cashews are a fat and calorie question. Macadamia nuts are a call-your-vet question. If your dog gets into a mixed nut bowl, macadamias are the ingredient to worry about first.

## How Many Cashews Are Safe?

PetMD gives size-based guidance, ranging from **one to two cashews for extra-small dogs (2–20 lbs) up to roughly 12 to 18 for extra-large dogs (91+ lbs)**.

A word of caution on the upper end: that number is a ceiling, not a serving suggestion. Eighteen cashews is a lot of fat for one sitting, and the same article warns about fatty foods and pancreatitis. Treat the low end of your dog's range as the sensible portion.

The rule underneath it all is the familiar one: **snacks should make up no more than 10% of your dog's daily calories.** Cashews are dense, so that 10% fills up fast.

## What About Allergies and Choking?

Two worries that come up often, both smaller than owners expect.

**Allergies.** PetMD says "the chance of your dog being allergic to cashews, or any other nut, is very unlikely," and cites a 2017 study finding that anaphylaxis to nuts has never been reported in dogs. What owners often read as an allergy is usually fat intolerance showing up as vomiting or diarrhea.

**Choking.** There is always some potential for a nut to be a choking hazard or cause an intestinal blockage. That said, PetMD points out cashews are softer than almonds and come without shells, which lowers the risk. Small dogs and enthusiastic swallowers are still worth watching.

## If Your Dog Ate a Whole Handful

This is the situation most owners are actually googling, usually after a bag got left on the coffee table.

A large serving of cashews is a large serving of fat, and the most likely outcome is an unhappy stomach over the next day — vomiting, diarrhea, gas, or a dog who seems generally off. Give them fresh water, hold off on the next meal if your vet suggests it, and keep the rest of the day bland.

Call your vet if you see repeated vomiting, a hunched or painful posture, weakness, or a swollen belly. Those can point toward pancreatitis or a blockage rather than simple indigestion, and both are worth catching early. Small dogs who ate a lot relative to their size deserve a call sooner rather than later.

Also check what kind of nuts they were. If macadamias were in the mix, treat that as the more urgent issue and call your vet regardless of how your dog seems.

## Practical Takeaways

- Cashews are **not toxic** — the concern is fat, not poisoning.
- Repeated fatty treats can lead to obesity and pancreatitis.
- Serve plain only: no salt, no seasoning, no honey roast.
- Stay at the low end of the size-based portion, and keep all treats under 10% of daily calories.
- **Macadamia nuts are the genuinely toxic nut.** Keep those away entirely.

A couple of plain cashews as an occasional treat is a reasonable thing to share. Just do not let it become part of the daily routine.
