## The Short Answer

**Yes, dogs can have cashews in small amounts — plain and unsalted.** Cashews are not toxic to dogs the way macadamia nuts are, so a stray one or two will not poison your dog.

The catch is fat. Cashews are rich and calorie-dense, which makes them a poor everyday treat. One or two plain cashews now and then is fine for most healthy dogs. A handful of salted cashews, or a scoop from a mixed nut bowl, is a different situation. Think of cashews as an occasional nibble, not something you keep in the treat jar.

## Why Cashews Aren't a Great Everyday Treat

Cashews do contain some genuinely useful nutrients — protein, healthy fats, magnesium, and antioxidants. But those perks come attached to a lot of fat, and that is the whole problem.

According to [PetMD](https://www.petmd.com/dog/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-cashews), the high fat content in cashews means that eating too many can lead to stomach upset and, over time, weight gain — and in dogs prone to it, fatty foods can trigger **pancreatitis**, a painful inflammation of the pancreas. A dog on a balanced diet gets everything it needs from its regular food, so cashews add calories without filling a nutritional gap.

That is why "safe" and "recommended" are not the same thing here. Cashews are safe in the sense that they are non-toxic, but they are not a treat worth reaching for often.

## The Real Dangers to Watch

Three specific things turn cashews from harmless to a problem:

- **Salt.** Most packaged cashews are salted, and the sodium is the bigger issue than the nut itself. In large amounts it can cause vomiting, weakness, tremors, and — at the extreme — seizures. Always choose plain, unsalted.
- **Mixed nuts.** This is the one that catches owners off guard. Snack bowls often mix cashews with **macadamia nuts, which are toxic to dogs.** The [American Kennel Club](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-nuts/) notes that macadamias can cause weakness, vomiting, tremors, and a fever in dogs. If your dog raided a mixed bowl, that is the risk to act on.
- **Choking and blockage.** Whole nuts can be a choking hazard for small dogs, and large amounts can be hard to pass. Cashew pieces are easier and safer than whole nuts.

## What About Cashew Butter?

A small lick of plain, unsalted cashew butter is usually fine — but the label matters. Some nut butters contain **xylitol**, a sweetener that is highly toxic to dogs even in small amounts, and others pack in added salt or sugar. Scan the ingredient list before you share any, and keep the portion to a taste rather than a spoonful.

## How Many Cashews Can a Dog Have?

Keep it tiny and occasional:

- **Small dogs (under 20 lbs)**: one plain, unsalted cashew
- **Medium dogs (20–50 lbs)**: one or two
- **Large dogs (50+ lbs)**: two or three

At most, offer these once or twice a week, and count them toward the rule that treats should stay under 10% of daily calories. The first time, give one and watch for any digestive upset before making it a habit. If your dog is overweight, prone to pancreatitis, or already gets other rich snacks, it is easy to skip cashews altogether — plenty of lower-fat treats like a piece of carrot or plain cooked chicken do the same job with far less risk.

## Signs Your Dog Had Too Many

Because cashews are fatty and often salted, an oversized serving tends to show up as:

- Loose stools or diarrhea
- Vomiting or a bloated belly
- Excessive thirst (a sign of too much salt)
- Lethargy or a tender abdomen

Mild upset usually passes within a day. Repeated vomiting, weakness, or tremors after a large salty portion is more serious and worth a call.

## When to Call Your Vet

Reach out to your vet if:

- Your dog may have eaten macadamia nuts from a mixed bowl
- Vomiting or diarrhea lasts more than 24 to 48 hours
- Your dog shows weakness, tremors, or excessive thirst after salted nuts
- Your dog ate anything containing xylitol

Plain cashews rarely cause an emergency, but the macadamia mix-up and hidden xylitol are the two scenarios where quick action matters.
