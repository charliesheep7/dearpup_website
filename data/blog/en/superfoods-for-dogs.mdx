Walk into any pet store and you will find kibble promising blueberries, kale, and salmon oil on the front of the bag. Some of that is marketing. Some of it is genuinely useful. Here is which foods earn the label, how much to actually serve, and the uncomfortable truth about what matters more than any of them.

## Do Superfoods Actually Do Anything for Dogs?

**Yes — but as a supplement to a complete diet, not a substitute for one.**

"Superfood" is a marketing word, not a scientific category. What it usually describes is a whole food that is unusually dense in vitamins, minerals, fiber, antioxidants, or omega-3 fats for its calorie count. The [AKC's guide to human superfoods for dogs](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/can-my-dog-eat-these-human-superfoods/) puts it plainly: these foods pack useful nutrients, and they belong on top of a nutritionally complete diet.

That last part matters. Your dog's base food should already meet AAFCO complete-and-balanced standards. Toppers are a bonus — and if they push out more than a small slice of the balanced diet, they become a problem instead of a benefit.

## 9 Superfoods Worth Adding

| Food                             | What it brings                         | Serving guide                                   |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| **Blueberries**                  | Antioxidants, vitamin C, fiber         | 3-5 for small dogs, a small handful for large   |
| **Pumpkin** (plain, cooked)      | Soluble fiber, beta-carotene           | 1-4 tablespoons per meal                        |
| **Salmon** (cooked, boneless)    | Omega-3s, protein                      | A few ounces, no more than once a week          |
| **Sardines** (in water, no salt) | Omega-3s, calcium from soft bones      | One small sardine for a medium dog, 2-3x a week |
| **Eggs** (cooked)                | Complete protein, choline, biotin      | Half an egg for small dogs, one for large       |
| **Carrots**                      | Beta-carotene, fiber, dental scrubbing | A few baby carrots or a raw stick               |
| **Sweet potato** (cooked, plain) | Fiber, vitamin A, potassium            | 1-2 tablespoons mashed                          |
| **Plain yogurt or kefir**        | Probiotics, calcium                    | 1-2 teaspoons small, 2 tablespoons large        |
| **Leafy greens** (kale, spinach) | Vitamins A, K, folate, iron            | 1-2 tablespoons chopped and cooked              |

A few notes worth having:

**Blueberries are the standout for aging dogs.** The [AKC notes](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-blueberries/) that the antioxidants in blueberries have been linked to improvements in cognitive function in older dogs and to joint and bone support in active ones. They are also low enough in calories that a handful barely dents the daily budget.

**Salmon must be cooked, and weekly is enough.** Raw or undercooked salmon can carry parasites and bacteria, so [cook it thoroughly](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-salmon/) and remove every bone. Once a week is the general rule of thumb.

**Pumpkin is the most useful thing in this list for digestion.** Plain cooked or canned pumpkin — never pie filling, which may contain spices and sometimes xylitol — is the standard first move for both loose stool and mild constipation, because soluble fiber works in both directions.

**Yogurt and kefir only if your dog tolerates dairy.** Many dogs are somewhat lactose intolerant. Start with a teaspoon and watch for gas or loose stool. If you are specifically after gut health, the [AKC has a fuller guide](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/dog-diet-gut-health/) to what actually helps.

## The 10% Rule That Keeps Toppers Safe

Everything above the base diet — treats, toppers, training rewards, the crust of your toast — should stay under **10% of daily calories combined**.

For a 30-pound dog eating roughly 700 calories a day, that is about 70 calories of wiggle room. A quarter cup of blueberries is around 20. One scrambled egg is about 80, which uses the whole allowance by itself. Two tablespoons of pumpkin is around 10.

Go past 10% regularly and two things happen: the balanced portion of the diet gets diluted, and calories creep. If you are not sure what your dog's baseline should be, our guide to [how much to feed your dog](/blog/how-much-food-should-i-feed-my-dog) works through it by weight and activity.

## The Habit That Beats Every Superfood

Here is the part the pet food marketing leaves out. The strongest evidence we have for extending a dog's life is not about any ingredient. It is about **portion size**.

A 14-year study of 48 Labrador Retrievers, reviewed in the [British Journal of Nutrition](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/diet-restriction-and-ageing-in-the-dog-major-observations-over-two-decades/3DDCC1DDF5A7D85518684AA687FBA63E), paired littermates and fed one dog in each pair 25% less food than its sibling for life. The results:

- **Median lifespan: 13.0 years for the lean dogs versus 11.2 years for the control group** — an extra 1.8 years
- **Hip arthritis at age 5: 13% of lean dogs versus 52% of control-fed dogs**
- High body fat and declining lean muscle mass both strongly predicted death

No blueberry has ever produced a result like that. If you add nine superfoods to your dog's bowl and they gain four pounds doing it, you have made a net-negative trade.

So the honest hierarchy looks like this:

1. **Keep your dog lean** — ribs findable, waist visible from above
2. **Feed a complete, balanced base diet** appropriate to age and size
3. **Then** add nutrient-dense whole foods within the 10% allowance

## What to Skip

Not every human health food translates. Keep these away entirely:

- **Grapes and raisins** — can cause kidney failure, with no reliably safe dose
- **Onions, garlic, leeks, chives** — damage red blood cells, cooked or raw
- **Macadamia nuts** — cause weakness, tremors, and vomiting
- **Avocado pit and skin** — choking and obstruction risk
- **Anything with xylitol** — a sweetener in some peanut butters and baked goods that is dangerous even in small amounts

The [AKC keeps a running list](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/fruits-vegetables-dogs-can-and-cant-eat/) of which fruits and vegetables are fine and which are not — worth a bookmark before you start improvising. Our own rundown of [human foods dogs can eat](/blog/what-human-food-can-dogs-eat) covers the same ground for pantry staples.

Also worth resisting: the urge to replace real food with pills. A balanced diet plus a few whole-food toppers covers most dogs, and unnecessary supplementation can push some nutrients past useful levels. If you think your dog has a genuine gap, [read up on canine vitamins](/blog/vitamins-for-dogs) and talk to your vet before adding anything.

## Practical Takeaways

- **Superfoods are toppers, not diets.** The base food does the heavy lifting.
- **Stay under 10% of daily calories** for everything outside the bowl.
- **Blueberries, pumpkin, sardines, and eggs** give the most nutrition per calorie and are easy to keep on hand.
- **Leanness beats ingredients.** A lean dog gained 1.8 median years and dramatically less arthritis in the best long-term study we have.
- **Introduce one food at a time**, in a small amount, and watch for 48 hours before adding another.
- **Check the avoid list** before offering anything new, especially anything marketed as healthy for people.

The most useful thing about the superfood conversation is that it gets owners thinking about food quality at all. Just aim that instinct at the whole bowl — including [eggs](/blog/are-eggs-good-for-dogs) and whatever else you are already adding — rather than at a single miracle berry.
