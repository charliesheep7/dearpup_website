## The Short Answer

Feed your adult dog **twice a day, in measured portions that add up to their daily calorie target** — not free-choice, and not whatever the scoop happens to hold.

The target itself is not a flat number per pound — small dogs burn noticeably more per pound than big ones — so getting it right takes two minutes of math and one month of watching your dog's waistline. The chart on the bag is a starting point, not an answer.

Here is how to get from a rough guess to a number you can actually trust.

## The Calorie Math Vets Actually Use

Veterinary nutritionists do not eyeball portions. They run two calculations.

**Step one: resting energy requirement (RER).** This is what your dog burns doing nothing at all. [PetMD](https://www.petmd.com/dog/nutrition/how-many-calories-does-a-dog-need) gives the standard formula:

> RER (calories/day) = 70 × (body weight in kg)<sup>0.75</sup>

Divide your dog's weight in pounds by 2.2 to get kilograms first.

**Step two: multiply for real life.** RER assumes a dog lying still. The maintenance energy requirement (MER) accounts for actually being a dog:

| Situation | Multiplier |
| --- | --- |
| Spayed or neutered adult | 1.6 × RER |
| Intact adult | 1.8 × RER |
| Needs to lose weight | 1.0 × RER |
| Light exercise | 2.0 × RER |
| Moderate exercise | 3.0 × RER |
| Heavy working dog | 6.0 × RER |

PetMD's worked example makes it concrete. A 50-pound neutered adult weighs 22.7 kg, giving an RER of about 728 calories. Multiply by 1.6 and you get **roughly 1,165 calories a day** — with a normal range of about 874 to 1,456, because individual dogs vary more than any formula admits.

That range is the important part. Two identical-looking Labradors can differ by several hundred calories a day. The formula gives you a starting point; your dog's body gives you the correction.

## Turning Calories Into Cups

Calories are useless until you convert them into something you can scoop.

Find the **calories per cup** (usually listed as kcal/cup) on your food's label — it is often in small print near the guaranteed analysis. Then divide your dog's daily calorie target by that number.

Our 50-pound example dog, eating a food at 382 calories per cup, needs about 3 cups a day. Split across two meals, that is 1½ cups morning and evening.

This is why [PetMD's feeding chart](https://www.petmd.com/dog/nutrition/are-you-feeding-your-dog-right-amount), built on a 382-calorie-per-cup food, gives these ranges for adults:

| Adult dog weight | Cups per day |
| --- | --- |
| 3–12 lbs | ½–1¼ |
| 13–20 lbs | 1¼–1⅔ |
| 21–35 lbs | 1⅔–2⅓ |
| 36–50 lbs | 2⅓–3 |
| 51–75 lbs | 3–3¾ |
| 76–100 lbs | 3¾–4⅔ |
| Over 100 lbs | 4⅔, plus ¼ cup per 10 lbs over 100 |

Swap to a denser food — many premium kibbles run 450 or more calories per cup — and the same dog needs noticeably less volume. That is the single most common reason a dog gains weight after a food change: the owner kept scooping the same amount.

Wet food shifts the math further, since it is mostly moisture. Our guides to [kibble](/blog/kibble-dog-food) and [wet dog food](/blog/wet-dog-food) cover how the two compare calorie for calorie.

## How Often: Two Meals, Usually

Once your dog passes their first birthday, the puppy schedule of three or four small meals can collapse into a simpler rhythm.

The [AKC](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/how-often-should-dogs-eat/) recommends twice a day as the default for adults, splitting the daily amount between morning and evening. [VCA Animal Hospitals](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/nutrition-general-feeding-guidelines-for-dogs) puts it slightly more loosely: once or twice per day suits most pet dogs, and many do well on two to three evenly divided meals.

Two meals is the practical sweet spot. It keeps a dog from going 24 hours between calories, spreads out the digestive load, and gives you two daily checkpoints where you notice whether they ate normally — which is often the first sign something is off.

### What about once-a-day feeding?

You may have seen headlines suggesting one meal a day is healthier. The source is real, and so are the caveats.

Survey data from the Dog Aging Project, covering **more than 24,000 dogs**, found that dogs fed once daily had lower odds of cognitive dysfunction and of several disease categories, including gastrointestinal, dental, orthopedic, kidney, and liver conditions. Only 8% of owners in the sample fed once a day.

But as the [AKC's coverage of the findings](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/feeding-your-dog-once-daily/) makes clear, the link is correlational, not causal. The researchers explicitly say owners should not change their dog's feeding schedule based on these results alone. Dogs who eat once a day may simply differ in other ways — household, activity, size, or owner habits — that the survey could not separate out.

Some dogs should definitely not be on a single daily meal: puppies, many seniors, diabetic dogs on insulin, dogs prone to bloat, and small breeds that can drop their blood sugar. ## Adjusting for Your Actual Dog

The formula gets you into the right neighborhood. These factors move you around inside it.

**Neuter status.** A spayed or neutered dog needs roughly 11% fewer calories than an intact one — the difference between the 1.6 and 1.8 multipliers. Many dogs gain weight after surgery for exactly this reason, because the portion never changed.

**Activity.** A dog who walks 20 minutes a day and one who runs trails on weekends are not on the same plan. Adjust seasonally, not just once.

**Weather.** The AKC notes that dogs exposed to severe cold may burn **10% to 90% more energy** than dogs in mild conditions. Outdoor working dogs in winter genuinely need more food.

**Size and breed.** Small breeds burn more calories per pound than large ones — the reason [small-breed formulas](/blog/small-breed-dog-food) are more calorie-dense per cup. Large and giant breeds have their own considerations around joint load and growth.

**Age.** Metabolism slows and activity drops in most senior dogs, though some older dogs actually need more calories as they lose muscle. [Senior dog food](/blog/senior-dog-food) covers how the requirements shift.

**Treats.** Everything outside the bowl counts. Keep treats, chews, and table scraps to no more than about 10% of daily calories, and subtract them from the meal portion rather than adding them on top. Our guide to [what human food dogs can eat](/blog/what-human-food-can-dogs-eat) is a good filter for what is worth the calories.

## The Only Check That Really Matters

Charts describe an average dog. Yours is not average.

Every two to four weeks, run the rib check the AKC describes: **run your hands along your dog's sides.** You should feel the ribs easily under a thin layer of cover, without pressing. Look down from above — there should be a visible waist behind the rib cage. From the side, the belly should tuck up rather than run straight back.

Then act on what you find:

- **Ribs are hard to feel, no waist** → cut the daily portion by about 10% and recheck in a month
- **Ribs are sharply visible, waist is dramatic** → increase by about 10%
- **Ribs easy to feel, clear waist** → you have the number right, leave it alone

Change one thing at a time and give it three to four weeks. Weekly portion tweaks just create noise you cannot read.

If your dog needs to lose weight, resist the urge to cut hard. Drop to the 1.0 multiplier under your vet's guidance and aim for slow loss — crash dieting risks muscle loss and, in some cases, nutrient shortfalls.

## Practical Takeaways

- Run the RER × multiplier math once to get a real starting number instead of guessing
- Convert calories to cups using the kcal/cup figure on your specific bag, and redo it after any food change
- Two measured meals a day is the sensible default for adults
- Treat package guidelines as a suggestion — the AKC warns they often overestimate
- The once-daily feeding research is interesting but correlational; do not restructure your dog's day around it
- Use a measuring cup, not a coffee mug or the scoop from the last bag
- Count treats inside the daily total, capped around 10%
- Re-check body condition monthly and adjust by 10% at a time

Feeding an adult dog well is not complicated, but it is specific. The dogs who stay lean into old age are usually the ones whose owners measured a portion once, wrote it down, and then quietly adjusted it twice a year for the next decade.
