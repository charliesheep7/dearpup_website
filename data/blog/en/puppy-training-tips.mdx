Bringing home a puppy feels like being handed a tiny, adorable houseguest who speaks no language you know. The good news: puppies learn fast. The better news: what you teach in the first few months shapes the dog they become.

Our [general dog training tips](/blog/dog-training-tips) cover the core principles that work at any age, like perfect timing and short sessions. This guide goes puppy-specific, walking you through what to focus on and when, from the day your puppy arrives to their first birthday.

## What You'll Need

- **Tiny, soft treats** that break into pea-sized pieces (puppies fill up fast)
- **A crate** big enough to stand up, turn around, and lie down in, but not much bigger
- **Chew toys** in a few textures for teething season
- **An enzyme cleaner** for the accidents that will happen
- **A marker word** like "yes" to pinpoint the moment your puppy gets it right

## Step 1: Use the Socialization Window (8 to 16 Weeks)

If you only prioritize one thing in the early weeks, make it this. According to [VCA Animal Hospitals](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/puppy-behavior-and-training-socialization-and-fear-prevention), puppies are primed for socialization **between 3 and 16 weeks of age**, a stretch called the sensitive period. Most puppies come home around eight weeks, so you get roughly two months of that window.

The [American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior](https://avsab.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/Puppy_Socialization_Position_Statement_Download_-_10-3-14.pdf) goes further. Its position statement says socialization should begin before a puppy is fully vaccinated, since the sensitive period ends before the vaccine series does. It notes puppies can start socialization classes as early as 7 to 8 weeks, as long as they've had at least one set of vaccines seven days before the first class.

What good socialization looks like:

1. **People of all kinds**: kids, tall people, people in hats, beards, sunglasses, and wheelchairs.
2. **Sounds**: the vacuum, doorbells, traffic, thunderstorms played quietly on a speaker.
3. **Surfaces**: grass, tile, metal grates, wobbly cushions.
4. **Handling**: gently touching paws, ears, and mouth so vet visits and nail trims feel normal.
5. **Calm, healthy dogs** that you know are vaccinated and puppy-friendly.

The key word is positive. Pair every new thing with treats and let your puppy approach at their own pace. If they back away or freeze, you've gone too fast. Make it easier and try again later.

## Step 2: Start Potty Training on Day One

Potty training is mostly about schedule, not discipline. The [AKC's potty training guide](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/training/how-to-potty-train-a-puppy/) points out that eight-week-old puppies have their weakest bladder control, and that a common rule of thumb is **about one hour of holding time per month of age**. A three-month-old may manage around three hours, though every puppy is different.

Take your puppy out:

- First thing in the morning and last thing at night
- Right after waking from a nap
- Within a few minutes of eating or drinking
- After play sessions
- Every one to two hours while they're awake, at the youngest ages

Go to the same spot each time, stay quiet and boring until they go, then mark and reward the instant they finish. If you catch an accident mid-act, calmly scoop them up and head outside. If you find one after the fact, just clean it with enzyme cleaner. Scolding teaches your puppy to hide when they need to go, not to go outside.

A steady feeding schedule makes potty timing far more predictable. Our [puppy feeding chart](/blog/how-much-to-feed-a-puppy-chart) can help you set meal times and portions.

## Step 3: Make the Crate a Happy Place

A crate isn't a punishment box. Done right, it becomes your puppy's den, a safe spot to nap, and a big help with potty training, because most puppies avoid soiling where they sleep. The AKC's [crate training guide](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/training/how-to-crate-train-dog/) walks through it gradually.

The short version: toss treats inside and let your puppy wander in and out with the door open. Feed meals in the crate. Close the door for a few seconds, then minutes, while you stay nearby. Build up time slowly, and always take them out to potty right after crate time. Keep crate stints within what your puppy can physically hold.

## Step 4: Teach Bite Inhibition Before Anything Else

Puppies explore the world with their mouths, and those needle teeth hurt. The goal isn't to stop mouthing overnight. It's to teach your puppy how soft human skin is. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/dog-care/common-dog-behavior-issues/mouthing-nipping-and-biting-puppies) calls this **bite inhibition**: a dog's ability to control the force of their mouth.

Puppies learn it naturally from littermates, who yelp and stop playing when bitten too hard. You can copy that:

1. Play gently with your hands.
2. When teeth press too hard, give a short, high-pitched "ouch" and let your hand go limp.
3. Pause play for a few seconds, then resume.
4. If your puppy bites hard again, end the game with a brief time-out.
5. Offer a chew toy instead, and praise them for chewing it.

Some puppies get more excited when you yelp. If that's yours, skip the yelp and go straight to calmly ending the game. Also watch for overtired "witching hour" biting. Often a nap in the crate fixes it faster than any training.

## Step 5: Teach Name, Sit, and Come

Once routines are rolling, add the first three cues. Keep sessions to a few minutes, a few times a day.

### Name

Say your puppy's name once in a happy voice. The moment they look at you, mark "yes" and treat. Repeat in different rooms. You're teaching that their name means "good things happen when you look at me."

### Sit

Hold a treat at your puppy's nose, then slowly raise it up and back over their head. As their bottom drops, mark and reward while they're still sitting. Add the word "sit" once they're doing it reliably.

### Come

Recall is the cue that keeps dogs safe, so make it the best deal in town. Crouch down, say "come" cheerfully, and back away a few steps. When your puppy reaches you, throw a mini party with treats and praise. **Never call your puppy to you for something they dislike**, like a bath or ending playtime, or the cue loses its magic.

For a deeper walkthrough of teaching cues step by step, see our guide on [how to train your dog](/blog/how-to-train-your-dog).

## First-Year Milestones at a Glance

Every puppy moves at their own pace, but here's a realistic rough roadmap:

| Age | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| 8-10 weeks | Potty schedule, crate comfort, name, gentle handling, safe socialization |
| 10-16 weeks | Bite inhibition, sit, come, puppy class, lots of new people and places |
| 4-6 months | Teething peaks, loose-leash walking, "down," "leave it," short stays |
| 6-12 months | Adolescence: proof cues around distractions, keep socializing calmly |

That last stage catches many owners off guard. VCA notes that **adolescence runs from about 6 to 18 months**, and it's a time to keep social experiences positive and calm. Your once-perfect puppy may suddenly "forget" their recall. That's normal. Go back a step, raise the value of your rewards, and stay consistent.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Waiting until after vaccines to socialize.** Talk to your vet about safe options so you don't miss the window.
- **Punishing accidents.** It creates fear, not house training.
- **Sessions that run too long.** Puppies have short attention spans. Quit while they're still keen.
- **Rough play with hands.** It teaches that hands are chew toys.
- **Letting "cute" behaviors slide.** Jumping up on you at 10 pounds looks very different at 60 pounds.
- **Forgetting mental exercise.** Bored puppies invent their own hobbies. Short [games for dogs](/blog/games-for-dogs) burn energy and build focus.

## Tips for Different Puppies

- **Small breeds** often have tiny bladders, so expect more frequent potty trips. Make sure they're socialized to being picked up and handled.
- **Large and giant breeds** get big fast, so prioritize polite greetings and loose-leash walking early.
- **Shy or sensitive puppies** need slower introductions and more distance from new things. If fear seems intense or isn't improving, check in with your vet. Our guide to [dog anxiety](/blog/dog-anxiety) covers early signs to watch for.
- **High-energy working breeds** thrive on short, frequent training sessions and puzzle play alongside physical exercise.

## The Takeaway

The first year goes by in a blur, but you don't have to do everything at once. Socialize thoughtfully before 16 weeks, keep potty and crate routines predictable, teach a soft mouth, and layer in name, sit, and come a few minutes at a time. Stay patient through the teenage months, and you'll end up with a confident, well-mannered companion.
