## The Short Answer

**Yes, dogs can have eggs — as long as they are fully cooked and served in moderation.** A plain cooked egg is one of the few human foods that brings real nutrition to the bowl instead of just extra calories.

The only version to avoid is raw. Cooking an egg removes the two things that make the raw version risky, and it takes about two minutes. Beyond that, the whole game is portion size: eggs are a treat, not a meal.

## Why Eggs Are Good for Dogs

Eggs are built around high-quality, complete protein — the same kind of protein your dog gets from meat. According to the [American Kennel Club](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-eggs/), eggs deliver essential amino acids that support muscle and tissue repair, along with fatty acids that help keep skin and coat healthy.

The yolk is the nutrient-dense part. It carries fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K, several B vitamins, and minerals like selenium and zinc. **A single large egg packs all of that into roughly 70 calories** — which is exactly why portion matters.

There is also a practical upside: plain cooked egg is gentle and easy to digest. Many owners reach for a spoonful of scrambled egg when a dog is a little off their food or recovering from a mild stomach upset.

## How Much Egg Can a Dog Have?

Eggs are a snack, not a protein replacement, so scale the portion to your dog's size:

- **Small dogs (under 20 lbs)**: about a quarter of an egg, two to three times a week
- **Medium dogs (20–50 lbs)**: half an egg to a whole egg, a couple of times a week
- **Large dogs (50+ lbs)**: one full egg, one to two times a week

The AKC notes that **very large dogs over 90 pounds can handle up to two eggs**, but even that stays in treat territory. The simple rule from [PetMD](https://www.petmd.com/dog/nutrition/can-dogs-eat-eggs) holds across the board: all treats combined should make up no more than 10% of your dog's daily calories.

If your dog is on a complete commercial diet, eggs are a bonus, not a nutritional requirement. Think of them the way you would a healthy snack for yourself — good in the right amount, a problem in excess.

## The Best Ways to Serve Eggs

Keep it plain and keep it simple. Safe preparations include:

- **Hard-boiled**: easy to portion, no add-ins, perfect for a quick treat
- **Scrambled**: fine with no butter, oil, salt, or seasoning
- **Poached**: just plain water, nothing else
- **Soft-boiled**: the yolk is cooked enough to be safe as an occasional option

Skip anything fried, cooked in butter or oil, or seasoned. The biggest hidden hazard is seasoning — onion and garlic (including the powdered versions that flavor most human eggs) are unsafe for dogs, so always cook your dog's portion separately.

What about the shell? Finely **ground eggshell is a natural calcium source** and is safe in small amounts, but only as a powder. Large or sharp fragments can irritate the mouth or throat, so grind them fully before sprinkling a pinch onto food.

## Why Raw Eggs Are the Exception

Cooked eggs are a green light. Raw eggs are the one form vets consistently steer owners away from, for two reasons:

### Salmonella Risk

Raw eggs can carry salmonella. Healthy adult dogs handle some bacterial exposure better than people do, but they are not immune — an infected dog can develop fever, vomiting, diarrhea, and lethargy, and can shed the bacteria in ways that reach the humans in the household. Cooking kills it.

### Avidin and Biotin

Raw egg whites contain avidin, a protein that binds biotin (a B vitamin) and blocks its absorption. Biotin supports skin, coat, and metabolism. An occasional lick of raw egg will not cause a deficiency, but feeding raw whites regularly can, over time. Cooking neutralizes avidin completely.

Both risks disappear the moment the egg is cooked, which is why "cooked only" is the standard advice.

## Signs Your Dog Had Too Much

Eggs are usually well tolerated, but watch for:

- Loose stools or diarrhea after eating
- Vomiting, most likely in a small dog who ate too much at once
- Reduced appetite or low energy

A whole egg eaten by a medium or large dog is very unlikely to cause trouble. Problems tend to show up with raw eggs given repeatedly, or with large portions given to little dogs.

## When to Call Your Vet

Reach out to your vet if:

- Vomiting or diarrhea lasts more than 24 hours
- Your dog seems genuinely unwell — fever, marked lethargy, or bloody stool
- You had been feeding raw eggs and notice coat or skin changes

For most dogs, eggs are one of the safest and most rewarding foods you can share. Cook them, keep the portion treat-sized, and they are a genuinely good addition to the rotation.
